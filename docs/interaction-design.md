# Interaction Design

This document owns UI and UX behavior, navigation, platform visual language, board presentation, help, animation and motion, visual effects, sound, haptics, accessibility, localization, and platform adaptation. It does not own product feature scope, Xiangqi rules, persistence formats, or engine behavior.

> **Status: binding.** Where a value is deliberately not fixed here — board dimensions, marker geometry, style colours, exact motion timings — the section says so and names what settles it. The Apple frontend's board dimensions live in `apple/MiniXiangqi/Board/BoardGeometry.swift`.

## Experience goals

- Make the board and current game state immediately understandable.
- Keep primary play actions easy to reach without obscuring the board.
- Provide a coherent experience across iOS, iPadOS, macOS, and Windows while respecting each platform's interaction conventions.
- Support learning through play: the interface should make legal moves, rules consequences, and results easy to understand for someone still learning the game they are playing.
- Treat accessibility as part of the design, not as a later visual adjustment.

## Navigation

The primary destinations are:

- **Play** for starting or resuming a game.
- **History** for reviewing and managing history records.
- **Settings** for user preferences.

Settings is five preference groups with an **About** page under them, which states the application's name, version, build and licence and links its source repository. **It carries exactly three group footers** — under **Confirm Before Deleting**, saying what turning the switch off costs; under **Confirm Before Placing**, saying what the switch does in both positions and which games it applies to; and under **Human versus AI Defaults**, saying what those two values are for — because a footer under every group is a screen nobody reads.

The navigation presentation must adapt appropriately to iPhone, iPad, Mac, and Windows. Platform adaptation may change presentation, but it must not create different product capabilities without an explicit product decision.

**The two board screens hide the destination bar where that bar stands across the bottom of the screen.** The board and [History replay](#history-replay) hide it while they are up; the three homes — the Play home, the History list, Settings — keep it, and so does a mode's pre-start page, [Custom Scene](#custom-scene) excepted under its own rule below. Leaving a board screen brings it back, so no destination is ever more than the page's own back control away and no capability is withdrawn.

**[Custom Scene](#custom-scene) hides it too, and wherever that bar exists rather than only where it stands across the bottom.** What the bar costs there is not height alone: a switch to another destination discards the position being composed, so the editor is a page that stands alone at every width. Its back control is the way out and brings the bar back.

**The condition is the presentation and not the device**, which is [Layout shapes](#layout-shapes)' own reasoning applied to the chrome instead of the board. That rule is the board screens'; it is about a bar standing at the foot of the screen, directly under the play controls and the replay transport, costing the page its full height; that is the **compact** presentation — an iPhone, and equally a narrow iPadOS window, which gets the same answer for the same reason. Where the container presents any other way it is left alone, because there the reclaimed space is not there to reclaim: an iPad at full width carries its destinations inside the navigation bar rather than beneath the controls, and hiding them returns **nothing at all** on the board and a single collapsed title row on replay, while taking away a visible way to the other destinations. **A Mac's sidebar is not a tab bar and this rule never reaches macOS**, where nothing about the window changes.

**Play has pages of its own.** Its root is the [Play home](#the-play-home), where what to play is chosen; each mode's pre-start state and the board are pages over it, reached by choosing and left by a back control in the toolbar, which names the page it returns to as the platform's own does. Leaving the board for the home ends nothing — the game stays active and the home's own card is the way back into it — and it is how a player reaches the mode entries while a game is going. A fresh launch opens **at the home**, in every mode and whatever is going: the active game is continued from the card there rather than entered automatically. Returning from the background is not a fresh launch and moves nothing.

**Titles say where you are, and their alignment is the platform's.** A destination's root carries the platform's large title — leading on iOS and iPadOS, in the toolbar on macOS — and a page walked into from one carries the inline title beside the control that walks back out, which on iPhone and iPad is centred and on a Mac is not. Every page in the app is one or the other — except the play and nearby boards in the stacked shape, whose bar centre carries the turn status in the title's place per [Turn status](#turn-status): a player looking at a board does not need the page named, and a resident status block above the board would spend on prose the height the bar already has room for. At the accessibility text sizes that send the status back above the board, the bar centre simply stays empty — the page still needs no name. Replay keeps its inline title, having no turn status to stand there. The app overrides the alignment of neither kind: which alignment a title takes belongs to the platform, and the same page's title reading differently on a Mac and on a phone is that rule working rather than an inconsistency. A large title is also most of the spare height the stacked layout has, which is the second reason a page over a board does not take one. **What the app owns is the page beneath the title**, and there the rule is its own: every text block on a page begins on **one leading edge** — 16 points in, the same inset the panel's sections take — in both layout shapes, so a page reads down one column whatever the title above it is doing.

## Platform visual language

Each platform uses its own current native visual system rather than an imitation of another platform's.

- On Apple platforms, Liquid Glass is the required material for functional interface layers — navigation, controls, toolbars, and contextual actions — and is not used in the content layer. It is required *of* those layers rather than maximised across the app; Apple's own guidance is to use it sparingly, and the board is content.
- On Windows, the WinUI 3 Fluent design system fills the same role: system materials, controls, and navigation patterns rather than recreated Apple styling.
- Preserve board readability and interaction clarity where translucent or material surfaces surround game content. They surround it and never overlap it, per the boundary below.
- Prefer platform-native behavior and adaptation over fixed imitations of one platform's layout.
- Visual effects must not make controls, state, focus, or text harder to perceive.
- The board, pieces, and game-state markers form one shared visual identity across platforms; only the surrounding functional chrome is platform-specific.

**Where glass may appear.** Custom glass belongs to the functional layer — the play controls, the result notice, and the replay transport — and is used sparingly rather than maximised. System-provided glass in the tab bar or sidebar, navigation bar and toolbar, alerts, sheets, context menus and History swipe actions is additional and automatic.

**Where it may not.** No **resident** surface may intersect the **board block**: the board core, which already includes the half-cell margin, together with the file-numeral strips. A **transient** surface may cover the board — a sheet the player asked for is expected to hide what is behind it while it is up, because they requested it and they dismiss it. The natural-result notice is a transient the player did not ask for, and it is dismissible for exactly that reason: it may stand in front of the board because the player can put it away, and the position it describes is underneath it undimmed the moment they do. Nothing in the content layer takes glass — the board surface, grid, palace diagonals, numerals, discs, symbols, and every game-state marker are drawn directly. The board's own background is a flat fill rather than a system material, because a translucent board would let whatever is behind the window shift the colour a player reads a piece by.

**Announcements and confirmations take different surfaces.** An announcement is the app's own: the result notice stands on custom glass over the board and can be put away, because it reports what has already happened and the player decides when to stop looking at it. A confirmation of a consequential act — the draw claim, **Resign**, **Save and Continue**, and the **Couldn't Save the Game** retry — is a system alert, presented wherever the platform puts one and blocking until it is answered, because the act itself does not happen until the player answers.

**The AI-thinking indicator carries no material at all.** It is present for a large share of every human-versus-AI game, and a persistent glass surface beside the board would be exactly the "gratuitous" application the guidance warns against.

**No tinted glass appears during play.** Saturated colour on the play screen then means one thing: which side a piece belongs to. Tint is reserved for a moment with a single obvious next action — **Resume Game** on the Play home while a game is going, **Start Game** in any pre-start state, **Save** on the result notice before confirmation, the concluding action the play-control cluster carries once a finished game's notice is closed, **Done** after it — and at most one tinted element is ever visible. The mode entries are not among them: they are a list of things to choose between, and neither of them is the answer. A system alert's own default action is tinted by the platform and stands above the page, so the at-most-one rule is counted in the app's own layer and never through a modal. Destructive actions use the system's destructive role rather than a red tint, so red keeps one meaning.

| Setting | System surfaces | Custom glass surfaces |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce Transparency | automatic | replace the material with an opaque fill and a hairline separator |
| Increase Contrast | automatic | keep the material, raise the container's border |
| Both | automatic | opaque fill **and** the raised border |
| Dark appearance | automatic | no change; the material adapts |

In every row the geometry, corner radius, and spacing are unchanged, so nothing reflows: what changes is the surface's own material and border, never its position or size. Apple publishes no specification for how Liquid Glass renders under Reduce Transparency, so these are our values rather than a documented behaviour.

## Piece representation

Xiangqi pieces are Chinese characters, and this is a teaching application, so the board presents the real characters rather than a translated substitute.

- The accepted piece characters give every piece type a distinct Red and Black form, so wherever the characters are shown the sides are distinguishable by glyph rather than by colour alone:

  | Piece | Red | Black |
  |---|---|---|
  | General | 帅 | 将 |
  | Advisor | 仕 | 士 |
  | Elephant | 相 | 象 |
  | Chariot | 俥 | 车 |
  | Horse | 傌 | 马 |
  | Cannon | 炮 | 砲 |
  | Soldier | 兵 | 卒 |

  Mini Xiangqi uses the five types in its rules; Xiangqi and Jieqi use all seven. Jieqi also draws a piece carrying no symbol at all, which is [The Jieqi board](#the-jieqi-board)'s.
- With Chinese characters selected, five pairs differ in whole shape. Advisor and cannon differ by a radical, and the cannon pair — 火 against 石 — remains the weakest at board size, so the piece style must carry additional non-colour distinction for it. 砲 is chosen over the equally attested 包 because it is the classical counterpart to 炮 and is the form the normative source itself uses in prose, though that source's summary table gives 包.
- Piece characters are game content, not interface text: they are identical in every supported language and are never translated on the board.
- On macOS every one of these characters resolves through the system font to a single Chinese family, at the matching weight from regular through heavy, with a uniform advance width — so pieces render at one consistent size without per-character adjustment. The equivalent confirmation on iOS and iPadOS is a required device check.
- When the icon symbol set is selected, both sides show the same symbol, so the symbol no longer distinguishes them and the piece style becomes the only non-colour carrier of side. Every accepted style must therefore satisfy the requirement below on its own, without help from the symbols.
- English piece names are General, Advisor, Elephant, Chariot, Horse, Cannon, and Soldier. They appear in help, accessibility announcements, and any descriptive text, never as board labels.

### Piece styles

The user chooses among three accepted piece styles, since a learner and an experienced player want different things from the same board. A style is a Settings preference and changes presentation only, never game content, archives, or the canonical notation. The three below are the contract the visual system is held to; the *control* that chooses among them is deferred until a second style is drawn, per the Settings scope in [product.md](product.md), and until then the board is Traditional.

A style covers the disc, its resting shadow, and **the board surface beneath it**, so each is one coherent look rather than pieces borrowed onto an unrelated board. What is drawn *on* the disc is the separate piece-symbols choice below, and every symbol set must work with every style.

- **Traditional** — the default. Both sides use the same warm light disc face, as a physical set does, with the symbols themselves in the Red and Black role colours. The Black disc additionally carries a heavier ring, so a second non-colour channel is always present. A soft resting shadow seats the disc on a warm low-chroma board surface.
- **Modern** — each disc is filled with a single strong colour, Red or Black, with a white ring inset within the disc and the symbol in white. It is flat, with no resting shadow, on a clean neutral board surface.
- **High Contrast** — Red is a filled disc with the symbol reversed out of it; Black is an outlined disc on a neutral face with a heavier ring. Structure and luminance carry the side as strongly as possible, for low-vision use. It is flat, so edges stay crisp, on a board surface tuned for maximum separation from both discs.

Each style therefore defines its own resting shadow, and there is no separate shadow setting. A resting shadow is presentation only: no style may rely on it to satisfy a contrast requirement, every requirement below must hold with the shadow removed, and resting shadows are reduced under Increase Contrast.

The lift shadow is different in kind and belongs to the motion language rather than to a style. A piece raised by selection or drag casts a larger, softer shadow in every style, because it communicates that the piece is held; it is never suppressed by a style choice, and Reduce Motion substitutes an immediate change for the animated lift rather than removing the state.

Every style must satisfy these, verifiable rather than aesthetic. They must hold **jointly**, not one at a time.

- **The sides stay distinguishable when hue is removed.** Each style names the channel that carries the side once the symbols no longer do, which is the case whenever icon symbols are selected, and that channel is structural or luminance-based rather than hue-based:
  - by disc fill, in Modern — the two fills reach at least 3:1 against each other;
  - by ring weight, in Traditional — the heavier ring is at least twice the width of the lighter, and each ring reaches at least 3:1 against its own disc face;
  - by construction, in High Contrast — filled against outlined is itself structural and needs no further threshold.
- **The symbol reaches at least 4.5:1 against its own disc face**, whether it is a character or an icon.
- **The disc's boundary — its ring or edge stroke — reaches at least 3:1 against the style's own board surface**, measured against that base surface rather than against a grid line, and at a point away from any board marking. The requirement is on the boundary rather than on the fill: a light disc on a light board is a legitimate traditional look, and what separates it is its edge.
- All of the above hold with resting shadows removed, in light and dark appearance, under Increase Contrast, and with either symbol set selected.

These requirements constrain each other, and Modern is the tight case: a white symbol needs a fill dark enough to reach 4.5:1, while the two fills must still differ by 3:1. Together those bound the Red fill within a narrow luminance band and force the Black fill very dark, so the Red must be somewhat lighter than the deep red of a physical set. Because the style's white inset ring, not its fill, is what separates the piece from the board, the style stays valid on a dark board as well as a light one.

The exact colour values, ring weights, and board surfaces are settled against a rendered board; this section fixes the constraints they must satisfy, not the values.

### Piece symbols

What appears on the disc is chosen separately from the style, because the two are independent: a learner may want icons on the traditional board, or characters on the modern one. The symbols choice is a Settings preference, changes presentation only, and composes with every style.

- **Chinese Characters** — the default. The accepted Red and Black characters above.
- **Icons** — a pictorial symbol per piece type, for readers who do not know the characters.

Rules for the icon set:

- Both sides show the **same** icon for a piece type, so an icon never carries the side. Side distinction rests entirely on the piece style, exactly as it must whenever the symbols stop differing, and every style already carries that requirement.
- Icons are drawn to match the discs, grid, and markers rather than imported from an unrelated visual language.
- Every icon is distinguishable from every other at the smallest supported board size. Chariot against cannon is the demanding pair, since both are long-range pieces with similar conventional forms, and the set is not acceptable until those two are unmistakable.
- Icons are game presentation, not interface text: they do not change with the interface language.

**The set is drawn for this project. It is original work, and this repository owns it.**

**The seven, and what each depicts.** A general's helmet, front view: a domed cap with a crest, standing on a band that flares below it. An advisor's ceremonial tablet. An elephant's head in profile. A chariot as a two-wheeled war cart in side view: a canopy overhanging the cart bed, and one wheel emerging beneath it. A horse's head in profile. A cannon as a barrel on a plain wedge, its muzzle raised. A soldier as an arrowhead over a flared base.

**The separation rule, which is how the chariot-cannon gate is met rather than hoped for.** Three independent channels carry the pair, so it survives losing any one of them:

- **The wheel belongs to exactly one piece.** The chariot carries the only circle in the set and the cannon carries no wheel at all. Any set that wheels both pieces loses the pair at our symbol size; the wheel-less cannon is an established variant of the convention rather than a departure from it.
- **The two envelopes differ in axis.** The cart is upright, the cannon is wide. An envelope is a property of the bounding box, so it survives being made small when internal detail does not.
- **The cannon is directional and the cart is not.** The cannon's mass sits at one end under a raised muzzle; the cart is drawn symmetric about its wheel, with no draw-pole, because a draw-pole would have made it directional too.

**Two rules of execution.** Icons are **solid silhouettes, never outline art** — line work at the symbol size turns to a grey smudge. And **internal detail is at least 2 points at the smallest supported board size, or it is not drawn**: spokes, mane hatching, and an eye all fall below that, so the wheel has one hub and no spokes and the horse has neither eye nor mane. Each icon is a single ink with knocked-out holes, so it recolours to the piece's role colour exactly as a character does and the 4.5:1 symbol-contrast requirement above is satisfied the same way.

**The reference sets, credited as references.** pychess-variants' `2dintl` and `eventintl` and its janggi `intlblue` and `intlwooden`, and the `euro` set below, were studied for what reads well at a small size and for which forms the convention actually uses. No path, outline, or file from any of them is copied, traced, or derived from.

**The fallback, named.** If the drawn set cannot hold the gate, the recorded fallback is pychess-variants' `euro` set — upstream `euro_xiangqi_js` from Sebastian Pipping's `xiangqi-setup` — under **CC BY 4.0**, by **Jasmin Scharrer and Sebastian Pipping**. Invoking it obliges us to credit both authors by name, name the licence and link it, and state that the work was modified, since adopting it means stripping its baked-in disc and converting its light detail overlays into holes. It is not what ships, and its forms — a crenellated tower for the chariot, a Latin cross on the general — are the reason.

## Board geometry and notation

Every game is played on the intersections of a line grid. Mini Xiangqi is 7 files by 7 ranks, 49 points; Xiangqi and Jieqi share one board of 9 files by 10 ranks, 90 points; Gomoku and Renju share one board of 15 files by 15 ranks, 225 points. The outer points sit on the border lines. No board is drawn as a checkerboard of squares, which would be wrong to anyone who knows the game and would teach a beginner the wrong mental model.

- Each palace is a 3-by-3 block of points. Mini Xiangqi's are `c1`–`e3` and `c5`–`e7`; Xiangqi's and Jieqi's are `d1`–`f3` and `d8`–`f10`. Their diagonals are drawn corner to corner at grid stroke weight.
- Mini Xiangqi has no river and its grid is unbroken. Xiangqi and Jieqi have a river between ranks 5 and 6: the horizontal banks remain, while the inner file lines stop at one bank and resume at the other.
- **The placement board is a plain grid**: no palace, no river, nothing broken. It prints the five star points a 15-line board carries — the four at the fourth line in from each corner, and the centre — as small filled dots in the grid's own ink. They are furniture of the board rather than markers on it: no game state is ever drawn in them, and a stone simply covers one.
- Starting points are not marked. They would compete with legal-move and last-move markers, which carry live information, and each fixed starting position is already visible when a game opens.
- Mini Xiangqi's grid is square, Xiangqi's is taller than it is wide, and the placement grid is square again. Each reserves a half-cell margin beyond the outer points so edge discs, stones and markers are never clipped. Edge labels sit outside that margin.
- **A stone is wider in its cell than a disc is**, as a real set's is, and still inside that cell, so neighbouring stones never overlap. A disc leaves room around itself for the air a character needs; a stone carries no symbol and needs none.

### Board metrics

Every board dimension is a multiple of the **cell pitch** `p`, the distance between two adjacent points, so every board scales without its relationships changing. Mini Xiangqi's accepted interactive floor is `p ≥ 44 pt`. Xiangqi keeps the same width footprint with nine files in place of seven and has the accepted floor `p ≥ 34 pt`. Its 90 hit elements are the actual disjoint `p` by `p` cells: they are never enlarged into overlapping 44-point targets, because one location must name exactly one point. A pre-start preview is not interactive and carries no floor — with one recorded platform exception, described under [Layout shapes](#layout-shapes).

**The placement games keep that same width footprint with fifteen files and have the accepted floor `p ≥ 20 pt`.** It is the shared footprint divided by their own file count rather than a floor of their own, and what the smaller cell costs is tap-target size and not legibility, a stone having no symbol to read. Their 225 hit elements are the disjoint `p` by `p` cells under the same rule, never enlarged.

The Mini Xiangqi core is `7 p` square. The Xiangqi core is `9 p` wide by `10 p` high. The placement core is `15 p` square. In each, the half-cell margins make the core dimension equal to the point count times `p`.

Three rules govern the space around a point. They are what allow one marker vocabulary to work on every piece style at every board size, and they are stated as relationships because relationships are what a reviewer can check.

- **Style decoration stays inside the disc; markers stay outside it.** On an occupied point, no game-state marker's ink touches the disc face, measured against the disc at its largest — a selected disc is scaled, and the rule holds at that size too. The air gap this leaves is what keeps a marker legible against Traditional's heavier Black ring, Modern's white inset ring, and High Contrast's outlined disc alike, and it is why the choice of characters or icons never affects a marker. Three markers are outside the rule because no disc is present to clear: the legal-destination dot and the drag-origin marker, which belong to empty points, and the pointer hover fill and the hint halo, which are drawn beneath the pieces.
- **Every marker stays inside its own cell.** A marker belonging to a point is contained within that point's `1 p` by `1 p` cell, at rest and throughout every animation, including the selection lift and any strengthening during a drag. Markers on adjacent points therefore cannot collide, in any position, without the pairing having to be checked case by case. The accepted half-cell margin is the same distance, so the outermost points' markers are contained by the margin and never reach the coordinates outside it. Where a marker would grow beyond the cell it grows inward instead. A dragged piece is the one exception, and only while it is dragged: it has detached from the grid and follows the touch across the board, so it belongs to no point.
- **Markers are never drawn in the Red or Black role colours**, which belong to the sides. Each style defines one **marker ink** per appearance, used at two strengths: **active ink**, at a contrast of at least 4.5:1, for selection, legal destinations, captures, check, and focus; and **record ink**, the same hue at reduced strength and at least 3:1, for the last move, the drag origin, and the destination markers a standing suggestion mutes. Beneath the pieces only, the same ink appears once more at low opacity — the pointer hover fill and the hint halo — carrying location and emphasis rather than state, which is why neither is held to a contrast floor of its own. Both strengths are measured against that style's own board surface, against the pointer hover fill composited over it, and against the hint halo composited over it for the marks that stand on the halo, with shadows excluded. Increase Contrast promotes record ink to active-ink values — except where record strength is itself the live distinction: a marker muted by a standing suggestion keeps its muted value, already at or above the record floor the promotion starts from, because promoting it would erase the one thing the muting says. Because every game-state marker is carried by luminance and shape rather than by hue, the board under Differentiate Without Color is identical to the board without it; the keyboard focus ring, which carries hue, is a platform affordance and never a game state.

**The exact dimensions are not fixed here.** Disc diameter, symbol size, every stroke width and radius, the grid weight, and the numeral strips' geometry are settled against a rendered board on real hardware, within the rules above and the contrast gates they carry; the Apple frontend's are in `apple/MiniXiangqi/Board/BoardGeometry.swift`. Fixing them in prose before anyone has seen them produces numbers that are internally consistent and unverifiable, which is worse than an honest gap.

### User-visible notation

The board edges and the move list render in the notation the **Notation** preference selects, in Mini Xiangqi and in Xiangqi; this section defines traditional Xiangqi notation, and [WXF rendering](#wxf-rendering) below defines the alternative. Jieqi's edges follow the preference beside them, and its move list does not, for the reason [The Jieqi board](#the-jieqi-board) gives. **Where nobody has chosen, the default follows the interface language: the traditional rendering under the Chinese interface, the WXF rendering under the English one.** Traditional notation is what Xiangqi instruction in Chinese actually uses, and a learner reading the Chinese interface should be able to carry what they read here into any Xiangqi book, video, or lesson; a reader of the English interface is by definition the reader the WXF rendering is for — one who has learned the game in the international notation rather than in Chinese — and opening them on a list of characters they cannot read teaches neither notation. The rule is *Chinese, or else WXF*, stated that way round so that a third interface language arrives with its answer already given: somebody not reading Chinese is not reading a Chinese move list either. **It is a default and never a migration.** The Notation preference overrides either, and a player who has chosen a reading keeps it whichever language they read the interface in, including where their choice is the other language's default. This is presentation only, whichever is selected: the canonical coordinate notation frozen in [xiangqi-rules.md](xiangqi-rules.md) remains what archives, fixtures, and the core interface store and exchange.

- Files are numbered from each player's own right, so the two sides number them in opposite directions: one through seven in Mini Xiangqi and one through nine in Xiangqi. Red writes its numbers as Chinese numerals and Black as Arabic numerals, and that applies to every number in a move, not only to the file. One word stands outside the rule: the ordinal that numbers four or more on a file continues 前, 中 and 后 rather than counting anything, and is written in Chinese for both sides.
- A move names the piece, its file, a direction, and a value. The directions are 进 forward, 退 back, and 平 across. The piece is named by its own side's character form from the table above.
- For the chariot, cannon, soldier, and general, 进 and 退 are followed by the number of ranks moved, and 平 is followed by the destination file. For the horse, advisor, and elephant, whose moves are not along a file, the value after 进 or 退 is the destination file.
- When two pieces of the same type stand on one file, the move opens with 前 or 后 **before** the piece name and omits the file — 前炮退二, not 炮前退二 — except when a second file is doubled, the last rule below. 后 names the piece nearer its own side and 前 the one nearer the opponent, a sense that is relative to the moving side and therefore unaffected by which way the board is facing.
- Three of a type on one file take 前, 中, and 后; four or more are numbered from the front — 一兵, 二兵, and so on, in Chinese numerals for both sides, because the ordinal is the positional word continued past three rather than a number in the move: Black writes 一卒进1, not 1卒进1. Both open the move and omit the file exactly as the two-piece form does. Neither is a curiosity here: five sideways-capable soldiers a side make each reachable.
- When more than one of the mover's files carries two or more of the same type — reachable only for soldiers, as 2-2 or 3-2 — the leading word alone no longer says which piece moved, so the origin file returns after the piece name: 前兵六进一, 后卒3进1. Pieces alone on their file keep the plain form, and this can never combine with the numbered form, which needs four on a single file. Of the forms beyond the rulebook's own 前, 中 and 后 — the sport's rulebook stops at three — the numbered series follows the computer standard's explicit clause, and the returned file is this contract's reading of established practice rather than a transcription of a normative source, recorded as such the way [xiangqi-rules.md](xiangqi-rules.md) records its adjudication readings.
- File numbers are shown in the outer margin. Ranks carry no labels, as they do not on a Xiangqi board. Because the two sides number the files oppositely, the margin follows the board's orientation so that the numbering beside a player is always that player's own.
- Traditional notation names pieces by their characters, so a user who selects icon symbols still reads a character-based move list under this notation. What that user is offered instead is the WXF rendering below, selected independently of the symbols: an icon reader who wants a letter-and-digit list has one, and a character learner keeps this one whichever symbols the board draws.

**On Windows the edges carry coordinates instead, for as long as the record does.** The Windows move record is the core's own canonical coordinate text — `d1d3` — and the strips exist so a player can map the record to the board, so a 一二三 edge beside that record would map to nothing a reader could follow. Windows therefore labels all four edges with the coordinates the selected game uses: files **a**–**g** and ranks **1**–**7** for Mini Xiangqi, files **a**–**i** and ranks **1**–**10** for Xiangqi. Ranks are labelled there and nowhere else, because canonical notation names a rank and traditional notation never does. A coordinate is absolute, so flipping re-orders the labels and never renames them. The board block no resident surface may intersect is the core together with all four strips. It supersedes [WXF rendering](#wxf-rendering)'s numeral-strip clause on that platform while Windows shows canonical moves.

### WXF rendering

The alternative the **Notation** preference offers, for a reader who has learned Xiangqi in the international notation rather than in Chinese. It is presentation exactly as the traditional rendering is, and it identifies pieces exactly as the traditional rendering does — in the mover's own frame, with the front of a file the end nearer the opponent, and with the identification applied whenever more than one of a type stands on a file rather than only when a move would otherwise be ambiguous — so a player who changes the preference sees the same move identified the same way in different clothes.

- **The shape.** A move is `[piece][origin][direction][value]`, with no spaces. The pieces are **K, A, E, R, H, C and P** — general, advisor, elephant, chariot, horse, cannon and soldier — one letter per type for both sides alike. Mini Xiangqi uses the five letters its piece set contains. Files are numbered **1 to 7 or 1 to 9 from each player's own right, in Arabic numerals for both sides**, which is where this rendering parts company with the traditional one's Chinese numerals for Red. The directions are **`+` advance, `-` retreat (an ASCII hyphen), and `=` traverse**.
- **The value.** If the move stays on its file, the number of ranks moved; otherwise the destination file. Stated as geometry rather than as a list of piece types, it covers the traverse and the horse without naming either.
- **Two of a type on one file.** The file digit is replaced by **`+` for the front piece or `-` for the rear, written after the piece letter** — `R+=3`, `P-+1`. Applied whenever two stand on the file, whether or not the other piece could make the same move. Reachable for chariots, horses, cannons and soldiers alike.
- **Three to five on one file** — soldiers only, since no other type has more than two. The piece letter is replaced by **the piece's index on its own file, counted 1 from the front**, and the origin file is kept: `15=4`, `25=4`, `35=4`, `45=4`, `55=4`. No letter ever appears beside an index.
- **Two files each carrying two or more** — soldiers only, as 2-2 or 3-2, and two such files is this board's maximum. Every soldier on one of those files takes the indexed form, counting from **its own file's** front and keeping the origin file: `13=4` and `23=4` beside `17=6` and `27=6`. A pair is promoted to the indexed form because a bare marker no longer says which file moved. Soldiers not on such a file keep the plain form.
- **The frame.** Everything is relative to the mover; front means nearer the opponent; index 1 is the frontmost.
- **No event markers.** No capture, check, mate, or result marker ever: a capture renders exactly as the quiet move would, identical to the traditional rendering's behaviour. So `+` in a move is only ever a direction or a front marker.
- **The numeral strips follow the preference.** The strips exist so a player can map the move list to the board, so with WXF selected both edges render Arabic numerals and with **Chinese** selected they are as defined above. Each side still numbers from its own right: Red's edge changes script, and nothing changes direction. This holds of the language-resolved default exactly as it holds of a chosen preference, because the strips follow the preference's answer and never a rule of their own: an English first launch draws both edges in Arabic numerals beside its WXF list, and a Chinese one draws Red's in Chinese numerals beside its traditional list.

Equivalently, as one condition: the indexed form whenever the type has three or more on the mover's file or two files carrying two or more; the marker form when exactly two stand on the file and it is the type's only doubled file; the plain form otherwise.

**There is no single WXF notation to transcribe, so the clauses above include recorded readings** in the same sense as the returned-file clause of the traditional rendering and as [xiangqi-rules.md](xiangqi-rules.md)'s adjudication readings. The federation's current rulebook and the federation's own long-standing notation page — the only WXF text that covers four and five on a file and two doubled files at all — contradict each other on marker order and on the traverse symbol, and the tandem cases have five mutually incompatible published readings. Two principles settle each choice: **one grammar**, whose four slots never migrate their meanings between clauses, and **the reader's WXF**, meaning that where the current rulebook stands alone against the federation's notation page, the computer standard, Wikipedia and both implementations, this contract follows the form the reader will have learned everywhere else. The readings, recorded:

1. **The marker follows the piece letter**, against the current rulebook's own marker-first examples, and with the notation page, the computer standard's explicit sentence on the point, Wikipedia and both implementations.
2. **`=` for the traverse**, from the current rulebook, against the notation page's dot — so the table takes its traverse symbol from one document and its tandem shapes from another, which their publisher never combined.
3. **The indexed forms at three or more and on doubled files**, generalising the rulebook's own three-soldier digits; the notation page's doubled `++`/`--` markers and the computer standard's cross-file letter series are both rejected as alien to every reader and implementation.
4. **The marker is unconditional**, on the rulebook's plain wording and on the traditional rendering's own behaviour, against one implementation's refinement of emitting it only where a move would otherwise be ambiguous, which no document authorises.
5. **The traverse value is the destination file**, implied by every example in every source and stated by none.
6. **The game's files.** Xiangqi uses the sources' files 1 to 9; Mini Xiangqi applies the same grammar to files 1 to 7.
7. **K and P against this interface's own prose**, which says General and Soldier. The letters are the standard's mnemonics, attested by both federation documents; G and S are attested by no WXF document and G collides with *guard* in the computer ecosystem's own set. Recorded as a tension and deliberately not reconciled: either substitution would break with every WXF-conformant reader and tool.

### Placement coordinates

Gomoku and Renju carry one convention of their own, and it is neither of the two renderings above. **The Notation preference does not apply to them and is consulted nowhere on their board or in their move list**: it chooses between two readings of a move that names a piece, a direction and a file numbered from a player's own right, and a placement game has none of those.

- **Letters along the bottom edge, numbers up the leading side**, in Go's own arrangement. The letters run `a` to `o` with nothing skipped and the numbers `1` to `15`, which is the core's own spelling of a point, letter for letter: the move list beside the board reads `h8`, and an edge that left `i` out would send a reader to the wrong line.
- **A move reads as the point the stone went on**, in both interface languages, because a coordinate is what the game names a move by rather than a rendering chosen for it.
- The board block no resident surface may intersect is the core together with both strips, exactly as it is the core together with the numeral strips on a xiangqi board.

## Board and game interaction

The board is the primary content during play. Its interaction design must cover:

- Color choice and the accepted board-orientation behavior below.
- Selection, legal destinations, the last move, check, and game result states.
- Move input, cancellation, repeated undo, and AI-thinking states.
- Clear prevention or explanation of unavailable actions.
- Replay controls and navigation through a history game.
- Help that explains the Mini Xiangqi concepts it currently covers and the
  shared interface behavior in context; a standard-Xiangqi rules reference is
  not part of the current Help surface.

### The Play home

**The Play destination's root is an independent page for choosing what to play, and there is no board anywhere on it.** What the player is doing on this page is choosing a game and a mode; a preview of a board they have not chosen yet answers no question they are asking.

- **A game is a headed section, and its ways to play are the rows in it.** The sections appear in this order: **Xiangqi**, **Mini Xiangqi**, **Jieqi**, **Gomoku**, then **Renju**. Each offers the ways to play that game: **Human versus AI** then **Free Play** where it has both, with [nearby play](#nearby-play)'s own row under them where that mode exists, and [Custom Scene](#custom-scene)'s last of all in the **Xiangqi** section alone. Selecting a row opens the pre-start state for exactly that game and mode.
- **The Jieqi section is two rows, not three**, because the game has no AI to offer: **Free Play** and then **Nearby Play**, which is the mode the game is for. Whether the **Free Play** row is shown to a reader at all is a product decision this document does not make; what the row opens, and what the board does when it is opened, is [The Jieqi board](#the-jieqi-board)'s.
- **With an active game the page also carries that game.** A **Current Game** card shows the accepted metadata line described under [Saving the active game before choosing a new mode](#saving-the-active-game-before-choosing-a-new-mode) — the same header, over the same line — and a prominent **Resume Game** that opens the board on the game exactly as it was left. Every fact on the line is read from the game the core is holding and none of it is re-derived.
- **A game already filed is not an active game**, and the card does not describe one. Its record is immutable History and the active-game reference was cleared by the terminal commit that made it; what stands on the board afterwards is the result where it was reached, which is presentation. Leaving that board for the home lets it go.
- The mode entries remain interactive whenever a game is active, and selecting one presents the accepted confirmation rather than opening anything. That flow is defined in full below.

### Starting and configuring a game

- A game is created by **Start Game** and by nothing else. Neither mode's first move creates one, and an untouched board persists nothing.
- With any active game, selecting any game-and-mode row immediately presents the one save-and-continue confirmation described below. This includes an ongoing game, a claimable but unclaimed neutral repetition, and an unconfirmed natural terminal result.
- Cancelling leaves the active game unchanged and does not enter the selected mode.
- **Save and Continue** first archives the active game according to its factual current state, then opens the selected mode's pre-start state. It does not create the new game.

The human-versus-AI pre-start state is not an active game:

- It visibly names the selected game above the mode-specific content. This is the same game name used by the Play-home section and metadata, not setup-only copy.
- It shows the initial board as a noninteractive preview and does not show a side-to-move status.
- A **This Game** control group offers **I Move First**, **AI Moves First**, and **Random**, plus **AI Level**. The first-mover control carries no visible label — the group header names the setup and the three options name themselves — so its own name, **First Mover**, is read only by a screen reader.
- The controls are initialized afresh from the persistent Settings defaults whenever the page is entered.
- Their values exist only as an in-memory draft. They are not autosaved, do not change the Settings defaults, and are discarded as soon as the user leaves the page.
- **Random** remains unresolved and previews Red at the bottom. Successful game creation flips the board only if Random resolves to **AI Moves First**.
- **Fast**, **Standard**, and **Deep** identify maximum AI thinking times of approximately 1, 3, and 5 seconds per move. **Standard** is the default on a new installation.
- **Start Game** creates no active game unless the required AI resources are available and the game can be persisted successfully. A Random first-mover choice is resolved only as part of successful game creation; if the resolved first mover is AI, search then begins.
- While creation is in progress, **Start Game** cannot be invoked again. Leaving invalidates the attempt, creates no game, and prevents a late completion from committing after the draft is discarded.
- Failed AI availability or active-game persistence keeps the page and draft available for retry, shows the applicable error, and re-enables **Start Game**. Leaving still discards the draft.
- There are exactly two applicable errors, and they are different messages under different titles. Insufficient memory — including an allocation that failed at a sufficient budget — is the accepted **Couldn't Start the AI Opponent** notice below. A creation the store would not persist is **Couldn't Start the Game** / **Saving the new game failed. Please try again.**, with **Cancel** and **Try Again**; it cannot borrow the **Couldn't Save the Game** wording, whose message promises that the current game is unchanged, because there is no current game to keep.

The Free Play pre-start state is also not an active game:

- It visibly names the selected game in the same place as human-versus-AI setup.
- It shows the same noninteractive initial-board preview with Red at the bottom and no side-to-move status.
- It explains **You control both Red and Black. Red moves first.**
- It shows **Start Game** without a **This Game** group, side selector, AI controls, or pre-start board-flip control.
- **Start Game** commits the active Free Play game, makes the board interactive, and then shows the Red side-to-move status.
- While creation is in progress, **Start Game** cannot be invoked again. Leaving invalidates the attempt and prevents a late completion from creating a game.
- A creation failure retains the pre-start page, presents an error, and re-enables **Start Game**. It creates no new Free Play game or further persistent change; an older game already archived through **Save and Continue** remains in History.

Settings has a **Human versus AI Defaults** group with **Default First Mover** and **Default AI Level**. Its footer explains that these values initialize future human-versus-AI setup and do not change an active game. A new installation selects **I Move First** and **Standard**.

**Where the concluding actions go.** A finished game's **New Game** — on the play-control cluster and, as **Save and New Game**, on the result notice — files the game and opens **that game's own mode's pre-start state**. It does not deal the next game: with an opponent to choose, the side and the level are chosen for each game rather than inherited from the last one, and a pre-start page is not a confirmation standing between the press and the new game. **Done**, on the recorded notice, returns to the Play home, where what to play is chosen again.

### Custom Scene

**Custom Scene** is the last row of the **Xiangqi** section on the [Play home](#the-play-home), under that game's ways to play, and it stands on iPhone, iPad and Mac. It is not a fourth way to play: what the row opens is an editor, and what the editor starts is an ordinary Free Play game of Xiangqi from the position composed in it. The row is a game-and-mode entry like every other, so with a game active it presents the accepted [save-and-continue confirmation](#saving-the-active-game-before-choosing-a-new-mode) and the editor opens only once that archive has committed.

The editor is a pre-start page over the home, left by the back control in the toolbar, and it creates nothing until **Start Game**:

- **The page stands alone.** It carries its own name — **Custom Scene** — in the title beside the back control, and it hides the destination bar under the rule in [Navigation](#navigation). The panel has no headline of its own; the page is named where a page is named. **Start Game** stands inside the safe area and never under a bar.
- **The board is empty and it is interactive.** It is this document's Xiangqi board, fitted by the rules under [Layout shapes](#layout-shapes), and because every point on it is a target it takes the interactive pitch floor rather than the exemption that section grants a pre-start preview.
- **The board says whose half is whose.** A small tag at each of its two outer edges, in that side's own ink on the board's own surface: Black's over the top half, Red's under the bottom. They are the editor's alone — a board being played says whose side is whose through the [turn status](#turn-status) and the orientation — and they take no input.
- **The palette holds the standard set** — every piece of both sides, **drawn exactly as the board draws them** — and each entry carries how many of that piece remain to place, with each side's row under a tag of its own. The entry the player picks is what the next tap puts down and reads as held; an entry with none remaining has nothing to offer, reads that way, and is not selectable.
- **A tap places and a tap removes.** Tapping an empty point puts the held piece on it, and tapping a piece already on the board takes it off and returns it to the palette.
- **A point that will not take the piece refuses it, with its reason.** Where the core reports the candidate position illegal in one of the classes that are about *where a single piece may stand* — the palace, whose reason for an advisor names the five points an advisor reaches rather than the palace it never left; an elephant's own side; a soldier's points, the rank and, before the river, the file — and reports the fault at the very point that was tapped, the piece does not land: the draft is unchanged, the piece stays held, its disc shakes off the point and goes, and the reason stands emphatically in the reason line while it does. The classes that are about the whole position — the two generals facing each other, the waiting side left in check — are placeable intermediate states a composer builds through, and the standing reason line is what reports them. No point is greyed out: a refusal that gives its reason teaches what a disabled board cannot.
- **The first mover is named and explained.** The choice carries a visible **First to Move** over it and one line under it saying that the side chosen makes the game's first move. That side is the one whose move the game's first ply is.
- **Validation is live and says one thing.** Where the position as it stands is not one to start from, the page carries one plain reason: the violation the core reports, in the order [xiangqi-rules.md](xiangqi-rules.md) states those clauses, with the count clause's answer for a board still missing a general worded as what to do next — place a general for each side; or a position that is a legal setup and already decided. Never a rule identifier, never a diagnostic, and never a second reason beside the first.
- **Start Game is enabled on a position that is both legal and playable**, and on nothing else: a position the side to move has no legal move in is already decided and is not a scene. Pressing it creates the Free Play game and opens the board on it.
- **The draft is in memory and nowhere else.** Leaving the editor discards it, and nothing about it is ever written.
- **A piece scales onto the board as it is placed and off it as it is removed**, on the app's own spring, and the reason line transitions rather than switching. Under [Reduce Motion](#motion-and-visual-effects) nothing moves: the discs arrive and go by opacity, and a refused piece says so where it stands instead of shaking.

In play the board page is the Free Play page and nothing on it is new: the same [play controls](#play-controls) — **Hint**, **Undo**, **Claim Draw**, **Flip Board** — the same [turn status](#turn-status), the same [markers](#game-state-markers), and repeated Undo back to the position the game began from. In [replay](#history-replay) the record opens at that position and steps through the game it became, and its [History](#history-library) row is every other row: what says where a game began is its own start position, and no marker is added for it.

### The Jieqi board

Jieqi is played on this document's Xiangqi board, and everything that board carries it carries: the grid, the palace and the river, the board metrics and the pitch floors, the maximum footprint, the edge labels, the [orientation](#board-orientation) with its **Flip Board**, the [markers](#game-state-markers), the [motion](#motion-and-visual-effects), and the [sounds](#board-play). The game adds three things to that board — a piece body, a reveal, and a surface for what has been captured — and takes the hint away. What a player may be shown is [jieqi-rules.md](jieqi-rules.md)'s disclosure section and never a judgement this surface makes.

- **A face-down piece is a third piece body**: the style's disc with its side ring and nothing on its face. It says whose piece it is and no more, which is the whole of what either player knows about it. The side is carried by the style's own non-hue channel, which is the case each style already answers for the icon symbols, since there is no symbol here to help carry it. Every accepted style draws this body, and its face is settled against a rendered board as every other visual specific in this document is.
- **A reveal is the piece turning up where it lands.** It is one event and not two: the piece travels its move in the ordinary language and arrives face up, so what is watched is a move the board answered rather than a move and then a flip. Nothing is drawn at the origin and no second beat is scheduled. Under [Reduce Motion](#motion-and-visual-effects) the identity arrives by opacity, which is that rule unchanged — opacity is not motion.
- **A capture is drawn as every capture is**, and what the capture *disclosed* stands in [Captured pieces](#captured-pieces). That surface is resident, so it never intersects the board block.
- **The turn status is the turn status.** The side-to-move line, the **Check** token, **Draw Available** where a claim stands, the acknowledgment beat, the save-failure capsule and nearby play's quiet asks all keep the grammar their own sections fix, with this game's own reason words in a finished game's secondary line, the forty-move draw among them.
- **The move list's rendering is deliberately not fixed here, and this is what settles it.** No convention for reading a Jieqi move exists to follow, so the rendering is designed rather than transcribed, and it is settled with the surface that draws it rather than in prose ahead of it. Two things bound it: the canonical coordinate form is what is stored and exchanged whatever is drawn, and a rendering may show only what the player reading it is entitled to know.
- **A point's description names what a player may know.** A face-down piece is announced by its side and as face down, never by the identity the position holds under it, so the reading order under [Accessibility](#accessibility) is unchanged and the vocabulary gains one word. Once the ending has disclosed a piece it is announced as the piece it is, like any other.
- **The end of the game turns every remaining piece up.** [jieqi-rules.md](jieqi-rules.md) discloses every hidden identity to both players by any ending, so the board's concealment ends with the game: each disc still face down carries the piece it was all along. It arrives as one settle at the moment the finished board arrives — after the ply that ended the game has finished being shown, which is the beat the result notice and the control cluster already wait for — and it arrives by opacity, so [Reduce Motion](#motion-and-visual-effects) draws it exactly as full motion does. In [replay](#history-replay) it is the final position that shows it: every earlier one is the game as it was played, and a walk back into it is a walk back into what the players knew then.

**Free Play is this game's public-state surface.** One person holds both hands there, so the board shows the public state whole — every fact both players hold — and nothing past it: a face-down disc stays face down until play turns it up or the game ends, though the position beneath it knows what it is, and both captured panels are visible at once. It is where the whole surface can be watched working, which is what that mode is for here.

### Board orientation

- In human-versus-AI play, the human player's side is displayed at the bottom. Choosing **I Move First** previews and resolves Red; choosing **AI Moves First** previews and resolves Black; choosing **Random** resolves the human side only when the game is created and then applies the corresponding orientation. **That is where the board starts and not where it must stay**: once the game is under way the visible **Flip Board** described under [Play controls](#play-controls) turns it over, and a new game starts the right way up again. The pre-start state offers no flip control — there is no game yet to look at from the other side.
- In Free Play, Red is at the bottom by default. Once the game starts, a visible **Flip Board** control allows the player to change orientation at any time.
- Human-versus-AI history replay defaults to the original human player's perspective. Free Play and imported history default to Red at the bottom. History replay provides the same visible orientation control.
- **A placement board has no orientation to change and carries no flip control**, in play, in nearby play, or in replay. A stone carries nothing a player could read the wrong way up, so there is nothing for a flip to correct; the board opens as it is drawn and stays there, whichever side the player took.
- Flipping the board changes presentation only. It does not change the side to move, game state, move history, or stored coordinates.
- Piece text and symbols remain upright and readable in either orientation.
- Board flipping uses a visible control rather than a hidden rotation gesture. The control has a localized accessibility label and an equivalent keyboard command where keyboard input is supported.

### Move input

**Tap is the input.** The drag-to-move design below is accepted and is kept here as reference; both commit through the same legal-move boundary.

- Tapping a movable piece controlled by the human selects it and reveals all of its legal destinations.
- Tapping a legal destination commits the move immediately. Ordinary legal moves do not require confirmation.
- Tapping another movable piece controlled by the human switches the selection directly.
- Tapping the selected piece again, or tapping outside the board, cancels the selection.
- Tapping an illegal board square does not move the piece or cancel the current selection. It provides the brief, non-blocking feedback defined under [Game-state markers](#game-state-markers).
- A legal move or an Undo whose immediate save fails does not happen: the board and game remain exactly at the pre-action state, brief non-blocking feedback distinct from illegal-move feedback indicates the change could not be saved, and the user may simply try again. There is no modal dialog and no accepted-but-unsaved change.
- When the failed save is the AI's reply, the game remains at the last committed position with the AI still to move, and the app requests a new AI move rather than asking the user to retry a move that is not theirs.
- A failed draw claim, resignation, or result confirmation uses the accepted **Couldn't Save the Game** retry presentation and leaves the game active and unchanged.
- Dragging a movable piece beyond the gesture threshold selects it and reveals its legal destinations. Dropping on a legal destination commits the move; dropping elsewhere returns the piece to its origin.
- iPhone and iPad use touch interaction. Mac and Windows support the equivalent click-to-move and pointer-drag behavior, and Windows touch devices support the touch interaction.
- Keyboard and VoiceOver use an equivalent select-piece, inspect-destinations, and select-destination flow rather than requiring a drag gesture.
- When input is unavailable, including while the AI is thinking or after a result is confirmed, the board rejects the interaction before visually moving a piece.

**On a board that places, the tap is the whole of the input.** There is no piece under the finger and no destination to reach, so the grammar above has no first step: a tap on a legal point puts the stone down, through the same committing gate every move commits through, and there is no drag.

- **A point that carries no legal placement offers nothing, and occupied and forbidden are one answer.** The board draws no destinations, so there is nothing to strengthen and nothing to say on the board at all; the acknowledgment beat on the turn-status element answers it, exactly as it answers any input the game cannot accept.
- **Settings offers an optional confirmation, off on a new installation.** With it on, the first tap marks the point, tapping the mark places the stone, tapping another legal point moves the mark, and tapping outside the board cancels it. It is never an alert and it blocks nothing, and it applies to the placement games alone.
- **A stone on the wrong point is recoverable everywhere** — by Undo in local play, and by the take-back negotiation in [nearby play](#nearby-play) — which is what makes the confirmation a preference rather than a protection.

### Game-state markers

The board shows the position and the states of the position, and nothing else. Anything the interface must say that is not a fact about the position — that a save failed, that input is unavailable right now — is said by the turn-status element instead, so the board is never read for two kinds of information at once.

Game states use four shape families, and no state borrows another's geometry: **small marks at a point** for things about a point, **rings around a disc** for things about a piece, **corner marks on a cell** for the last move, and **the turn-status element** for things about the game. Within each family every member has a distinct shape — filled against hollow, solid against dashed, single against double — so no two game states are confusable at any supported size, on any piece style, in either appearance, with either symbol set. The pointer hover fill and the keyboard focus ring sit outside these families and are deliberately rectangular: they report where an input device is, never what the game is doing. The hint halo sits outside them too — a wash beneath the pieces rather than a marker among them, carrying emphasis for a suggestion whose state the strengthened marker above it already carries.

- **Selected piece.** The disc lifts and casts the lift shadow, and a solid ring is drawn around it in active ink, attached to the piece. Lift and shadow may not carry selection alone — shadows weaken under Increase Contrast, and a small scale change is not absolutely readable — so the ring is what makes the state certain.
- **A pending stone.** On a board that places, a point marked for a stone that is not down yet takes that same solid ring in active ink, standing on the empty point rather than around a disc: nothing is on the board until the mark is confirmed, so the ring is the whole of the mark. It is what the optional placement confirmation raises under [Move input](#move-input), and what a shown hint puts at the point the engine chose.
- **A forbidden point.** A small cross in record ink at every point the side to move may not play — Renju's forbidden points, on Black's turn. A cross because it must be unmistakable against every circular mark the board draws, and because it is what a renju board prints; record ink because active ink is what says *you may*, and a prohibition drawn in it would compete with the affordances rather than qualify them. Every forbidden point is empty by the rule that forbids it, so the cross never falls on a stone.
- **Legal empty destination.** A filled dot in active ink, centred on the point and covering the grid crossing. Deliberately small: a chariot on an open board offers a dozen of these at once, and they must read as an available path rather than as clutter.
- **Legal capture.** A dashed ring in active ink around the enemy disc, reaching the cell boundary. The target does not lift; only the piece the player holds lifts. Solid ring against dashed ring is the shape distinction the accepted piece-style requirement asks for, and in any case the two never appear on one disc — one rings the player's piece, the other an opponent's.
- **Last move.** Four L-shaped corner brackets in record ink on both the origin cell and the destination cell, inset from each cell corner so that two adjacent cells' brackets stay visibly separate — a one-step move puts them side by side. Angular where every other marker is circular, so they are unmistakable at any size. They always mark the move that produced the position on screen: an Undo moves them to the move that is now last, replay moves them as it steps, and no brackets appear at an initial position. The AI's move uses these same brackets rather than a second marker.
- **General in check.** A double ring in active ink around the checked general, shown whenever that side is in check and the general is not held. It pulses once as it appears and never again; the pulse animates stroke weight rather than scale, which would carry the ring out of its cell.
- **A held general in check.** The selection ring and the check rings occupy the same band and cannot both be drawn. While a checked general is held — selected or dragged — the check rings hide; they return the moment it is released, including when a drag is abandoned. During play a **Check** token in the turn status carries the state through that gap, so check is never invisible while the general is in the player's hand. In replay no piece is ever held, so the rings alone carry it there.
- **Illegal tap.** No board mark. With a piece selected, its legal-destination markers pulse once: the question a learner is asking is where this piece may go, and answering it teaches more than marking the rejected point. Under Reduce Motion they change state once instead of pulsing, so the answer still arrives without animation and without depending on haptics a Mac does not have. With nothing selected, the turn status gives the acknowledgment beat described below. Either way the platform's lightest selection-weight feedback fires where the hardware provides it, never the warning pattern, and the selection is retained.
- **Dragged piece.** The disc detaches, scales up with the strongest lift shadow, and follows the touch or pointer directly. On touch platforms it is offset above the touch point so that a fingertip never covers it; under a pointer there is no offset. The origin keeps a hollow dot in record ink — the vacated twin of the filled destination dot, unambiguous against it because an origin is never itself a legal destination. A legal point near the drag strengthens, and releases again as the drag moves away, with enough hysteresis between the two distances that it cannot flicker along a cell boundary.
- **A shown hint.** The suggested piece is taken up exactly as the player's own tap takes one up — the selection ring, the lift, and every legal destination — and the suggestion is then said three ways — one across the whole set, two at the suggested point, every mark inside its own cell. **The rest of the set mutes**: every destination marker of the held piece except the suggested one drops to record ink for as long as the suggestion stands, so the suggested marker is the one active-ink marker the selection shows and finding it is a glance rather than a comparison. **The suggested marker strengthens** by exactly the amount the illegal-tap answer strengthens the whole set by, arriving with one swell and then holding. **And the point carries the halo**: a soft circular wash of active ink at low opacity, drawn beneath the pieces as the pointer hover fill is, filling the cell beneath an empty point's dot and, around a capture target, the marker band behind the dashed ring — whatever space the point has free. The halo is emphasis rather than state and is the one mark the board draws below record strength: the strengthened marker and the muting are what carry the state, in weight and luminance rather than hue — so the board without the halo still says which destination the engine chose, Differentiate Without Color changes nothing, and Increase Contrast leaves the muting standing, per the marker-ink rule. A drag's nearby strengthening applies to a muted marker at its muted strength — proximity never unmutes — and on the suggested marker nothing compounds: the illegal-tap answer and the suggestion share one ceiling, and the marker takes the stronger of the two rather than their sum. **A suggested capture's dashes drift**: the ring rotates slowly, a revolution in roughly ten seconds, for as long as the suggestion stands — the one continuous motion the board ever draws, permitted because the player asked for the state it marks, and ended by everything that ends the suggestion. Under Reduce Motion the swell is skipped, the dashes hold still, and every standing mark arrives and remains unchanged. See [Hints](#hints).
- **Pointer hover — on Windows.** The point under the pointer takes a faint rounded-square fill in marker ink at low opacity, drawn beneath the pieces. It reports where the pointer is, not what is legal. A hover never previews a piece's legal destinations: selection already answers that question in one click, and a preview that appeared under the pointer would answer it before the player asked, which is the opposite of how the hint they *can* ask for works. **The fill belongs to Windows alone.** A faint fill under the pointer is the Fluent idiom's own answer to where a pointer is, and the Mac's board deliberately stays quiet. The layering order below and the marker-ink measurement above still name the fill, and name it where it exists.
- **Keyboard focus.** The focused point takes a rounded-square outline in the platform's focus colour, clearing even a selected disc at full lift and staying inside the cell. It is the one marker that carries hue, because matching the platform's own focus ring is worth more here than vocabulary purity, and its rectangular shape distinguishes it regardless. It is also the only marker permitted to cross another: it is a platform affordance drawn above the board, and it never carries game state by itself.

Two states deliberately have no board marker at all.

- **A failed save on the user's own action** — a move or an Undo — is reported by a transient capsule anchored to the turn-status element, reading **Couldn't save that move. Please try again.**, with the system warning pattern reserved for genuine failures. It is distinguished from illegal-tap feedback structurally — a different surface, a different shape, a different feedback pattern — rather than by inventing a second board mark. The board shows nothing because the position did not change. When the failed save is the AI's reply no capsule appears and the board shows nothing at all, because the retry is the app's to perform and not the user's. This capsule is for a single ply; a failed draw claim, resignation, or result confirmation uses the accepted **Couldn't Save the Game** retry presentation instead.
- **Unavailable input** is answered by an acknowledgment beat on the turn-status element: its background rises to full emphasis and falls back, in opacity only, with no movement, plus the same lightest feedback as an illegal tap. The reason input is unavailable is always already on screen, so the beat points at it rather than repeating it. The board is never dimmed while the AI is thinking or after a result is confirmed: a board with nothing wrong with it should look like a board, and the pause while the AI thinks is exactly when a learner wants to study the position. In replay the board is a read-only document, and a tap on it does nothing at all.

**Layering**, from the board upward: the style's board surface; the grid and palace diagonals; the pointer hover fill and the hint halo; last-move brackets; destination dots and the drag origin; resting discs with their style resting shadows; rings around resting discs; the held or dragged disc with its lift shadow and attached selection ring; the keyboard focus ring. Rings are drawn above resting discs so that no disc can clip one.

Because every marker is contained by its own cell, only markers on the *same* point can ever meet, and those cases are closed: a destination dot or a capture ring coexists with last-move brackets without touching them, since the brackets occupy the cell's corners and the rings pass through its edge midpoints; a capture ring never surrounds a checked general, because no position a player can reach offers a general as a legal capture target; last-move brackets never fall on a checked general's cell, because the move that produced the position was the opponent's, so its origin is now empty and its destination holds the opponent's own piece; and a held general in check is resolved by the rule above.

**The exact geometry is not fixed here** — stroke widths, radii, the dash pattern, the lift and drag scales, and the strengthening distances are settled against a rendered board, within the containment and air-gap rules above.

### Play controls

A small, calm cluster of controls sits together during play, so that it stays reachable under a thumb and never competes with the board for width. What bounds it is that judgement rather than a count: the controls must not crowd the screen or read strangely, which is settled against a rendered screen rather than fixed as a number in prose. The accepted compositions are:

- **Human versus AI** — **Hint**, **Undo**, **Claim Draw**, **Resign**, and **Flip Board**. The flip is the same presentation-only control Free Play and replay carry, under the same key and the same word. **It takes no tint**, and the cluster's one tinted moment is the concluding action.
- **Free Play** — **Hint**, **Undo**, **Claim Draw**, **Flip Board**. It cannot resign, having no opponent to resign to.
- **Nearby** — the acts of whoever's turn it is, then **Resign** and **Flip Board**, per [Nearby play](#nearby-play). It has no hint.
- **Replay** — the transport controls, and **Flip Board** where the record's game has an orientation to change. Replay offers no move input, so no play control applies.

**The placement games carry fewer of these, and each absence is a capability that is not there rather than an act that is momentarily impossible.** They have no repetition to claim — every ply adds a stone and none is ever removed, so no position of one occurs twice — and no orientation to change, so **Claim Draw** and **Flip Board** are absent from every cluster of theirs: human versus AI is **Hint**, **Undo** and **Resign**; Free Play is **Hint** and **Undo**; nearby is the acts of whoever's turn it is and **Resign**, its own turn leading with **Accept** while a peer's request stands and with nothing when none does; replay is the transport alone. A finished placement board is **Undo** and the concluding action in either local mode, and the concluding action alone in nearby play.

**Jieqi carries all of these but the hint**, and that absence is a capability that is not there rather than an act momentarily impossible: nothing in the app plays the game, so there is nobody to ask what they would play. Its Free Play cluster is **Undo**, **Claim Draw** and **Flip Board** — the game has a repetition to claim and an orientation to change, both of them Xiangqi's — and its nearby sets are the nearby sets unchanged, which carry no hint anywhere. A finished Jieqi board is the finished scene as every other board's is.

**The replay cluster is a grid.** Its five transport controls are one row of equal cells with one gap between them, and **Flip Board**, where the record's game carries one, spans beneath it the width the row spans, so the cluster's leading and trailing edges are the header's and the move list's. One gap, five equal cells, one width.

**The cluster is a scene's fixed set, and inside a scene nothing comes or goes.** A scene is a stretch of play whose acts are stable — a live local game, one side's turn of a nearby game, a finished board — and its composition above is the whole of what it shows. A control whose act is momentarily impossible is **disabled where it stands, never removed**: Hint greys through the machine's turn, Claim Draw greys until a claim stands, Undo greys while a transition runs. A greyed control is a promise the scene keeps — it will be pressable again without anything moving — where a control that leaves and returns makes the row a different row each time a thumb reaches for it. Absence stays what says a **capability** is not there at all: Free Play carries no Resign and nearby play no Hint, and no mode's cluster shows another mode's acts.

**A scene change is one drawn transition.** When the composition changes — the turn passing between the two nearby sets, a game finishing anywhere — the controls the two scenes share hold their identity and their places, departing controls fade as their width closes, and arriving ones fade in, riding the landing of the move that changed the scene. A scene change no move produced — the claim, a resignation, a nearby game ending without a result — draws the same transition at the moment the act is confirmed, which is the moment its conclusion already sounds. In nearby play **Resign**, and **Flip Board** where the game carries one, stand at the trailing end of both sets and therefore never move at all; the negotiations change on the leading side, and **Accept** takes the claim's own place while something stands, per [Nearby play](#nearby-play). Under Reduce Motion the sets crossfade, nothing sliding and nothing collapsing.

**Every control carries one symbol, the same in every mode**: a lamp for **Hint**, a return arrow for **Undo**, the equality sign for **Claim Draw**, two figures hand in hand for **Offer Draw**, a check mark for **Accept**, the flag for **Resign**, the paired arrows for **Flip Board**. The platform's symbol set has no handshake, and the pair is the nearest form of the same thought; it never shares a row with the equality sign — a claim and an offer belong to opposite sides of the turn — so the two cannot be taken for each other. The concluding action is the one control that is its word alone — it is the moment's single tinted act, and a word is what a moment like that gets.

**Words and symbols degrade together.** Where a row holds every control with its word, every control carries it; where it cannot, **every control in the row gives up its word at once** and stands as its symbol, with the word as its accessibility label — no row mixes a worded control with a symbol-only one. An arrangement may still wrap, **Flip Board** dropping to a second row where a panel's width will not hold one, and a wrapped cluster answers the question row by row: replay already stands that way, its transport symbol-only by [History replay](#history-replay)'s own clause over its worded **Flip Board**. The concluding action stands outside the fallback — it has no symbol and keeps its word in any row, because the moment's single tinted act is named rather than depicted. A scene change re-answers the arrangement for its new set, and a shared control may arrive worded where it stood as its symbol, riding the same transition. The arrangement is the layout's answer and not a promise: what is promised is that every control of the scene is on screen and inside the surface carrying it — the panel's own inset edge side by side, the space's full width stacked — at the smallest size each platform allows. The symbols, the uniform fallback and the drawn scene transition are accepted for the Apple frontend; Windows keeps its column arrangement of these compositions — without Hint, which awaits that platform's own hint work — and adopting this grammar there is recorded register work.

A finished board is a scene of its own: there is no draw left to judge and no hint left to ask, so the finished cluster is **Undo**, the concluding action, and **Flip Board** where the game carries one, in both local modes, for as long as the game is finished — **Resign** goes with the game it could have ended, and the change arrives as the scene transition above.

**Resign** presents a confirmation, since it ends the game against the player and cannot be undone:

- Title: **Resign?**
- Message: **Resigning records this game as your loss.**
- Actions: **Cancel** and **Resign**.

Confirming records a human loss and moves the game to immutable History. Cancelling changes nothing. Resignation stays a separate confirmed action and is never folded into the natural-result notice.

### Turn status

A persistent status element near the board is one coherent description of the current play state:

- Its primary line always identifies the side to move, in that game's own words: the localized equivalent of **Red to Move** or **Black to Move** on a xiangqi board, and of **Black to Move** or **White to Move** on a placement board, where the side that moves first is Black and the stones are what the two sides are called by.
- In human-versus-AI play, a secondary label identifies that side's controller as **You** or **AI**; in [nearby play](#nearby-play) the same label names the other player as **Them**. AI thinking is shown as activity attached to the AI's turn; it does not replace or compete with the side-to-move line. The controller label belongs to a turn and stops when the turn does: a finished game is nobody's to move, and the secondary line then carries the result's reason instead.
- **The AI activity slot is a small system activity indicator beside the AI controller label.** It appears only once a search has run long enough to be worth showing — an indicator that arrives and leaves inside a third of a second reads as a flicker rather than as thinking — it never replaces the side-to-move line, it carries no material at all, and it is gone when the reply lands.
- The same slot carries the stalled state when the engine could not be prepared mid-game and the player answered **Later**: **The AI can't start right now** with **Try Again** beside it. See [Insufficient memory for AI play](#insufficient-memory-for-ai-play).
- The hint control and its activity are the play-control cluster's, defined in full under [Hints](#hints).
- Free Play omits a human/AI controller label because the same person controls both sides.
- While the side to move is in check, a **Check** token accompanies the side-to-move line for as long as that remains true. Replay has no side-to-move line and therefore no token; there the board's own check treatment carries it, which is sound because replay never holds a piece.
- The element carries the two transient board-state messages that are not facts about the position: the save-failure capsule, and the acknowledgment beat that answers input the game cannot accept. Both are defined under [Game-state markers](#game-state-markers). In Free Play the element's **Draw Available** line is half of the standing draw-claim offer, alongside the enabled **Claim Draw** control.
- The design does not add a separate, unrelated instruction such as "please move" or "your turn."
- Board input is accepted only when the committed game state permits the user to move. It is disabled while the AI is thinking.
- History replay uses a separate move-progress and playback state rather than describing the position as a human or AI turn.

**Where the element stands follows the layout shape.** Beside the board it is the panel's first section, which is already the top. In the stacked shape it is the page's bar centre — the title's place, which a board page does not spend on a name — the side-to-move line as its primary text with the secondary label beside it, and the **Check** token and the activity indicator riding inline. The element keeps its duties wherever it stands: the acknowledgment beat raises the bar element's own background, and the lines the bar cannot hold — the save-failure capsule, the stalled slot and its retry, nearby play's quiet asks — hang just beneath the bar, in front of the air above the board — the element's own anchoring, wherever it stands. At accessibility text sizes the bar cannot hold the element at all and it returns to its place above the board, sized as the measured chrome it is. The bar-centre placement is the Apple frontend's; Windows keeps its current arrangement until the board-page catch-up the register records.

Turn ownership, activity, and input availability must not be communicated by color alone. The element is driven by committed game and engine state, and a resolved Random side choice is visible in game metadata.

### Hints

The player may ask the engine what it would play. What arrives is a suggestion and never a move: the board shows it, and only the player's own tap makes it a move.

- **The control is a play control**: the cluster's leading member in both modes that have hints, under the same glass as its neighbours, carrying the lamp and its word by the cluster's own rules under [Play controls](#play-controls). It leads because it is the one control about the next move rather than about the game around it.
- **It is present throughout the scene and disabled when no hint is possible right now** — greyed through the machine's turn, pressable again on the player's own, per the cluster's own grammar; a finished board's scene has dropped it entirely. Possible means: a game an engine plays, human-versus-AI or Free Play, the game live, and the turn the player's own — the human's against the AI, either side's in Free Play. Never in [nearby play](#nearby-play) and never in Jieqi, where the whole capability is absent rather than a greyed promise.
- **The presentation is the board's own language**, defined under [Game-state markers](#game-state-markers): the suggested piece is taken up exactly as a tap takes one up, and the suggested destination is marked as that section defines — the muting, the strengthening, the halo, and the capture drift. Tapping that destination commits the move through the ordinary input path, and every ordinary selection interaction applies unchanged — switching to another piece, tapping the held piece again, tapping outside the board.
- **On a board that places, that language is the pending stone**: no piece is taken up, because there is none to take up, and the suggestion is the mark standing at the point the engine chose, with the halo beneath it. Tapping the mark plays it through the same input path a mark the player raised themselves is played through, and moving or cancelling it is that flow's own — which is why the presentation is the same whether or not the placement confirmation is on.
- **Activity is shown only past the threshold the AI's own activity waits out**, and in the same form: a small system activity indicator standing in the control's own symbol's place until the search answers, carrying no material.
- **A suggestion is dismissed by anything that makes it untrue or answers it**: playing it or any other move, an Undo, a selection that moves elsewhere, cancelling the selection, the game ending, leaving the board. A repeated request on an unchanged position re-shows the same suggestion rather than thinking about it again, and any change to the position discards it.
- **Silent, per the sound contract.** Selection makes no sound, and a suggestion is a selection the app made; the haptics are whatever the selection behaviour already provides and nothing further.
- **VoiceOver hears the suggestion when it appears**, announced rather than focused, so the reader keeps the point they were on. The announcement is composed from the point vocabulary under [Accessibility](#accessibility) — the side, the piece, and the two point names, as in **Suggestion: Red Cannon b1 to b5** — and the suggested point's own description gains a **Suggested** state token beside the existing **Selected** and **Legal Move** vocabulary.
- **Nothing about a hint is recorded**, per [product.md](product.md): the game, its History record, and every export are what they would have been.
- **A hint the engine could not be prepared for** presents the accepted insufficient-memory shape under its own title — **Couldn't Get a Hint**, because the reader asked for a hint and Free Play has no opponent to fail to start — over the same message as the pre-start notice, with **Cancel** and **Try Again**, and every retry re-probes fresh. Only the memory failures take that message: any other preparation failure is a damaged installation, and it takes the same title over the hint's own **A hint isn't available right now. Please try again.** — cause-free, since naming the wrong cause is worse than naming none, and opponent-free, since the mode that asked may have none.

### Insufficient memory for AI play

- Before an AI engine is initialized, the app calculates the accepted Hash budget. A budget below 256 MiB does not start the AI opponent.
- The app presents a notice with:
  - Title: **Couldn't Start the AI Opponent**
  - Message: **There is not enough memory available. Please close some other apps, then try again.**
  - Actions: **Cancel** and **Try Again**.
- **Cancel** dismisses the notice without creating or changing an active game. In pre-start setup, the in-memory draft remains while the user stays on that page.
- Retrying obtains a fresh value from the platform memory probe and recalculates the budget; the prior value is not cached.
- This low-memory path does not substitute a smaller Hash or perform a special automatic cleanup pass to manufacture the minimum budget.
- Any active game being resumed remains saved and unchanged while the AI opponent is unavailable.
- The notice may suggest closing other apps, but it does not promise that the operating system will terminate them or that a retry will succeed.

**Mid-game, the same situation keeps its name and gains a guarantee.** When the app returns from suspension owing a search and preparation fails, the alert keeps the title **Couldn't Start the AI Opponent** — the situation is the same one, memory is not available right now, and a second title would name a distinction the reader cannot act on — but its message adds the one thing the pre-start case has no need of: **There is not enough memory available. The game is saved and you can continue later. Please close some other apps, then try again.** Its actions are **Later** and **Try Again**, not **Cancel** and **Try Again**, because there is nothing to cancel. After **Later** the stalled state lives where things about the game live — the turn status's AI activity slot, with an inline **Try Again** — and every retry re-probes fresh. Undo remains available throughout and is itself a way out: removing the human's last move returns the game to their decision point, where no search is owed and the stalled state has nothing left to be about.

### Saving the active game before choosing a new mode

The [Play home](#the-play-home) shows the active game's metadata under a **Current Game** header and a direct **Resume Game** action beside it. The metadata begins with the game and mode, then identifies the human's side when applicable and the move count. It shows the side to move for an ongoing game, the result and reason for a terminal game, and claim availability when applicable. The three state classes are exclusive and each takes one shape: an ongoing game is **In Progress** and whose turn it is; a claimable repetition is **In Progress** and **Draw Available**, the standing offer taking the side-to-move slot; a terminal game is its result and the reason for it, in the same longer register the History row uses.

Every game-and-mode entry remains interactive whenever an active game exists. Selecting one temporarily remembers that destination in memory and immediately uses one fixed confirmation for every old-game, old-mode, new-game, new-mode, and active-game-state combination:

- Title: **Start a new game?**
- Metadata header: **Current Game**
- Message: **This game will be saved to History as it is now.**
- Actions: **Cancel** and **Save and Continue**.

Only the metadata changes; the title, message, and actions do not interpolate game-, mode-, or state-specific wording. Example metadata lines include **Xiangqi · Human versus AI · You: Red · In Progress · Black to Move · 42 moves** and **Mini Xiangqi · Free Play · Red Wins · Checkmate · 42 moves**. The metadata reports facts and does not ask the user to classify the result.

**Save and Continue** applies the classification automatically:

- An ordinary ongoing game is archived as ended early without a competitive result.
- A neutral threefold repetition that is only claimable and has not been claimed is still ongoing, so it is archived as ended early rather than as a draw.
- An unconfirmed natural terminal game keeps its actual winner or draw and its exact termination reason.

The confirmation does not add **Undo** or **Claim Draw** actions. **Cancel** discards the temporarily selected destination and leaves the active game completely unchanged; the user can resume it and use the board's normal Undo or draw-claim controls.

Archiving and clearing the active game must commit atomically before navigation. On success, the selected mode's pre-start state opens, and no new game exists until **Start Game** succeeds. If the user leaves that pre-start state, the archived game remains in History and no active game is created. The archive is the one operation on this path that is **not** driven from the UI thread — [core-interface.md](core-interface.md)'s threading contract keeps `mxq_store_archive_and_clear` outside the main-actor exception the active game's own commits run under — so the confirmation's answer is not the same instant as the archive's.

If persistence fails, the old active game remains unchanged, the selected pre-start state does not open, and no new game is created. The exceptional error uses:

- Title: **Couldn't Save the Game**
- Message: **The current game is unchanged. Please try again.**
- Actions: **Cancel** and **Try Again**.

The requested destination remains temporary only while this confirmation or retry flow exists. Cancelling the error discards it; retrying repeats the same atomic archive operation.

### Undo and result confirmation

- Free Play removes one move per Undo action and can repeat back to the initial position.
- In human-versus-AI play, Undo while the AI is thinking cancels the search and removes the human move that triggered it.
- After the AI has replied, one Undo action removes the AI reply and the preceding human move, returning to the previous human decision point. The action can be repeated by complete decision cycles. How many plies a decision is, is the core's answer and never a count taken above it.
- **A decision cycle rewinds as one gesture**: both discs travel back together in a single transition, and whatever either of them took reappears as they go. It is one action, so it is one travel and one arrival; drawn as two reversals in sequence it would be two, and the second would have to be stopped from being read as a separate Undo. Where the reply took the very piece that invited it, the two reversals share a square, and the restored piece belongs to the one carrying that piece home rather than to both.
- If a human move itself reaches a natural terminal state, Undo removes that human move while the result presentation remains unconfirmed.
- If the AI moved first, its opening move alone cannot be undone.
- Redo is not available. A new move after Undo permanently replaces the discarded continuation.
- A natural result remains undoable while its result presentation awaits confirmation. Undo dismisses that presentation and resumes the game.
- After result confirmation, resignation confirmation, or **Save and Continue**, the History record is immutable and cannot be undone.
- Undo is disabled at the earliest valid boundary and while a prior Undo transition is still being applied.

### Natural result presentation

- When a natural terminal result is reached, a result notice appears in front of the board. Nothing else changes: the final board stays exactly as the last move left it, undimmed, with the notice the only thing added to it.
- The notice title is the localized equivalent of **Red Wins**, **Black Wins**, or **Draw**. A second line explains the result reason. Human-versus-AI play may also show relevant player metadata without replacing the result.
- **Before confirmation, both actions save, and the default one only saves.** **Save** confirms the result, files the game in History, and leaves the board standing exactly at the result it reached. **Save and New Game** does that and resets the board in the same press. **Save** is the notice's default and the tinted one: when a game ends, keeping the game that was just played is wanted far more often than being dealt the next one, and a notice whose one prominent action clears the board away is a notice arguing with its own reader.
- **Undo is not on the notice.** It is a play control, and it stays in the play-control cluster, where it is available for exactly as long as the result is unconfirmed — including after the notice has been closed, because closing saves nothing. Offering one action in front of the board and again behind it teaches nothing about either. Undo itself is unchanged: it follows the mode-specific behavior above, takes the notice with it, and resumes the active game.
- The notice is dismissible — by its own close control, by the platform's cancel key, or by a tap on the board — and it does not present itself again for the same result. Closing it decides nothing about the game: the result is still carried by the turn status, and the concluding action is still carried by the play-control cluster, whose finished scene it belongs to for as long as the game is finished. The squares a result is worth studying are usually the ones a notice standing over the board would cover, which is why it can be put away.
- A terminal game whose notice was closed without concluding is still archived with its actual winner or draw and its exact termination reason by the save-and-continue flow above, so dismissing the notice never loses the record.
- Resignation remains a separate confirmed action rather than being folded into the natural-result notice.
- After **Save**, the final board remains visible, the record becomes immutable History, and the notice changes to **Saved to History** where it stands. That is the whole of the change on screen: result, save, recorded, in one place, with the position that produced the result still under it.
- The recorded state offers **Replay**, which opens the newly created History record from its initial position, and **Done**, which returns to the Play home.
- **A game already filed is never filed again.** A claimed draw arrives at its notice already recorded, and so does a result the player has saved; the concluding action on either of them resets the board and commits nothing further.
- There is no Play Again action on this notice.
- The play-control cluster's concluding action stays **New Game**, and no confirmation stands between it and the new game: it files the finished game and resets the board, which is exactly what the notice's second action does under a name that says so.
- On macOS the two actions sit side by side, with the default one trailing, where this platform puts a default action. **iOS keeps them side by side too**: at iPhone width, **Save and New Game** sits beside **Save** in either language, the longer English included, without crowding the notice or reaching the screen's edges. Permission to stack them stands for a size that has not been met.

### Claimable threefold repetition

- In both human-versus-AI play and Free Play, a neutral threefold repetition does not automatically end the game.
- The claim is the player's to invoke. **Claim Draw** presents the blocking notice — title **This position has occurred three times.**, message **You can end the game as a draw.** — which offers **Keep Playing** and **End as a Draw**. The split is the accepted presentation and the two are never recombined into one sentence in the title.
- **End as a Draw** confirms an immutable draw record in History, and presents the claimed draw through the result notice without **Undo**: the claim is the player's own confirmed act, and it is the one finish that cannot be walked back.
- After **Keep Playing**, the same still-valid claim is exposed through a non-blocking **Draw Available** affordance instead of repeatedly presenting the same blocking notice. In Free Play the standing offer is the enabled **Claim Draw** control together with the turn status's **Draw Available** line, and nothing blocks the board.
- **The notice never presents itself unbidden, in either mode.** It is the confirmation of the player's own act — **Claim Draw** — and a confirmation that presents itself inverts the accepted announcement/confirmation grammar under [Platform visual language](#platform-visual-language): announcements self-present and are dismissible; confirmations follow an act and block until answered. So human-versus-AI play uses exactly what Free Play accepts — the enabled **Claim Draw** control and the turn status's **Draw Available** line — even though there the repetition can arrive on a move the player did not choose. One vocabulary across both modes, and the claim stays the player's to invoke.
- Claiming while the AI is thinking is legal exactly when the core reports the claim available. Where it is, the search is cancelled before the terminal commit: a search outstanding over a game that has just ended answers to nothing.

### Nearby play

Two people near each other, two devices, one game, over the [BoardGame Protocol](boardgame-protocol-v2.md). The protocol decides what may be said between the devices; this section decides what is on screen.

- **The entry is the last row of each game's section on the [Play home](#the-play-home)**, **Nearby Play**, under the ways to play that game locally — its third row where the game offers both of them, and Jieqi's second, that game offering no **Human versus AI**. It exists on iPhone and iPad and never on macOS. The row stands wherever either way of reaching another device could carry a game, which on those two is always; where none could, it is **absent rather than disabled**, a row that can never be pressed being a promise the hardware cannot keep, and no explanation helping a reader who cannot change the answer. The row opens the nearby game already going in that game, exactly as the **Current Game** card opens a local one; with none it raises that game's propose sheet.
- **The room is everybody there is to play with** — a device this one is paired with, and a device on the same local network running the application. Each is one row, one device, in one list; a device that is both is one row and not two; and **no row says which of the two it is**, the player choosing somebody to play rather than a way of reaching them. **No words the app writes ever name a transport, a band, or a path** — not a row, not a status line, not a refusal, not the sentence it gives a system prompt to explain itself by.
- **A device the system holds no name for is called Nearby Device** — one label for all of them and never an identifier, carrying four characters of the device's identity wherever that identity offers any, which is a disambiguator rather than a name and is there whether or not a second such device is in the room, a label that appeared and disappeared as other devices came and went being a label that changes under the reader.
- **The room has two sources, and only one of them sleeps.** A paired device is listed from the system's pairing record, whether or not a connection stands to it and whatever the other device is doing, because a pairing is a standing fact about the pair. A device that is not paired is listed while it is on the network with its own player in nearby play — which lasts as long as a nearby surface is up there or anything is still owed to somebody — so a device is in the room because its player is playing rather than because the application is installed on it. **The invitation is available where the chosen device holds a live connection**, and waits where it does not.
- **A game is the same game however the two devices reached each other.** The board, the negotiations, the settling, the result, the record, and coming back into an interrupted game are one behaviour, and no surface differs by which way a game is carried — including a game that begins one way and continues the other.
- **Pairing is the system's**, through its own device-pairing interface, once per pair of devices, and it sits on the propose sheet because it is the same errand. Either device may offer itself or look for the other. **What pairing buys is reach**: a device paired here is in the room wherever the two are, and a device that is not is in it while the two share a network. Where the system's pairing views cannot be offered at all, **This device can't pair** stands in their place, so the sheet says why the errand is missing rather than dropping it.
- **The permission the local network needs is the system's own, asked in context.** The first use of nearby play raises it and nothing before that does, over the surface the player has just opened, with a plain sentence saying what it is for. Declined, nearby play is exactly what it is without it — the same sheet, the same board, the same rules, a shorter reach — and the app neither asks again nor explains nor refuses anything on its account. What a refusal costs is the devices the network would have added to the room, and a room without them is a room with fewer people in it, which needs no saying.
- **A proposal is the row's game, one side, and one device.** The game is not chosen again — the row named it — and what the sheet carries is the side the proposer takes, **I Move First** or **They Move First**, the devices in the room, and **Send Invitation**. While an invitation is unanswered the sheet says so and offers no second one.
- **Jieqi is one of the games a nearby proposal names**, and it is the mode that game is for. Its deal is invisible: the two devices settle it between accepting and the first move, so what a player sees is a board arriving a moment later than another game's would, with nothing to answer and nothing to read. The machinery is the protocol's, and no screen in this document reports any part of it.
- **The other player consents.** An arriving proposal presents a system alert naming the device, the game, and the side **this** device would take, with **Accept** and **Decline**; accepting opens the board. It presents over whichever destination is showing, because a session belongs to the peer rather than to a page, and it is a confirmation of a consequential act, which is a system alert by [Platform visual language](#platform-visual-language). **It is the same alert whatever device the proposal came from**, one this device is paired with or one it has never met: consenting to the game is the whole gate, and there is no second one.
- **A refusal is presented by its reason.** Every reason the protocol carries, and every refusal this device's own engine makes, has a sentence of its own. No wire code and no diagnostic reaches the reader. Its title says which of the two it is — a game that never began, or one that was under way and is not any more.
- **The nearby board is the play screen's board**: the same two [layout shapes](#layout-shapes), the same [turn status](#turn-status), the same [markers](#game-state-markers), the same motion and the same sounds. The board accepts a tap only while the session is bound to a connection and the ply is this device's; off-turn it is quiet and unmarked, and a tap it cannot accept takes the acknowledgment beat like any other. **Resign** and **Flip Board** are on it throughout, the way out replacing **Resign** once the game is over.
- **The negotiations belong to whose turn it is**, because the protocol's own do: an offer and a request are the off-turn player's to open, a claim is a turn action, and an acceptance answers something that arrived. Off turn the cluster carries **Offer Draw** and **Undo** — the take-back being of that player's own last move — and on turn it carries **Claim Draw**, with **Accept** in its place while the other player has something standing. Each is available exactly when the session engine allows it and disabled otherwise, which is the grammar the cluster already keeps for **Claim Draw**. The two turn compositions are two scenes of the one cluster, per [Play controls](#play-controls): **Resign**, and **Flip Board** where the game carries one, stand at the trailing end of both and never move, and the turn passing swaps the leading acts through the drawn scene transition.
- **Which of those two the game carries is the game's**, per the same section. A placement game has neither a claim nor an orientation, so on its own turn the leading side carries **Accept** while the other player has something standing and nothing when they do not, and **Resign** stands alone at the trailing end. The off-turn set is unchanged: a draw offered between two people is meaningful in a game with no repetition to claim, and a take-back is how a misplaced stone comes back.
- **The claim is the local game's claim.** Where the engine says it stands for this device's player, the enabled **Claim Draw** control and the turn status's **Draw Available** line are the standing offer, and pressing it presents the same confirmation in the same words as [Claimable threefold repetition](#claimable-threefold-repetition). Claiming ends the game as the rules-decided draw, through the result notice like any other end.
- **What the other player asked for is a line, not a prompt.** **They offer a draw** or **They want to take back a move** stands in the turn status's own quiet register beside the **Accept** that answers it, and nothing blocks the board: playing on is the other answer, and it needs no control, because a move voids what was standing and takes the line and the control with it. An accepted draw ends the game as **Draw by Agreement**; an accepted take-back is shown with the board's own Undo motion.
- **The board says nothing about the connection in ordinary play.** Connections drop between moves and are restored beneath the game; neither is reported, and nothing about either is modal. One line, in the turn status's own quiet register, appears where the link is costing the player something — what they did has not reached the other device, or their own turn has been blocked past a real stretch — and it withdraws when the link returns.
- **Leaving the board ends nothing.** The session stands until the game does, and its own row on the Play home is the way back in.
- **A nearby game is the library's one active game, and is come back into through the [Play home](#the-play-home)'s **Current Game** card like any other.** It survives a relaunch: the card is there, and the way in rebuilds the session and continues it. Starting a game of any mode over it presents the accepted [save-before-mode](#saving-the-active-game-before-choosing-a-new-mode) confirmation, and that includes starting a nearby one — a proposal is not sent, and an invitation not answered, until there is room in the library for the game it would create.
- **A ply of the player's own the library would not keep is reported by the accepted brief save-failure feedback**, and by nothing else. What it reports is not a move to try again: a nearby ply has already reached the other device and stands there whatever this device's library did.
- **A finished nearby game files itself.** It enters History the moment the two devices have settled on how it ended, rather than by a filing act on this one: a result one player confirms and the other does not would be two libraries disagreeing about one game.
- **A finished nearby game presents the accepted result notice**, with **Done** as its one action. A rematch is a fresh proposal, so nothing offers one.
- **A game that ends without a result says why, once.** The other device no longer holding it, the two devices ceasing to agree about it, and the other player starting a new one are the three ways it happens, and each is one sentence in that same notice, over **The Game Has Ended**. The board stays where it is behind it — the position is still worth looking at — and nothing else is said: not the connection, not a second sentence, and never anything about what the app did next.

### History replay

- Replay entered from History or from the just-recorded result begins at the game's initial position.
- The board is read-only. Replay does not offer move input, Undo, or starting a new game from the displayed position.
- Controls provide jump to beginning, one move back, play or pause, one move forward, and jump to end. They are icon-only and carry their words as accessibility labels: five labelled buttons do not fit beside a board, and a transport is the one place a glyph is the familiar form. The end controls take the media transport's own symbols and the single steps take chevrons, because a step is not a scan.
- The move list highlights the currently displayed move and allows the user to jump to a selected move. The highlight is a filled shape and a heavier weight, never colour alone.
- Replay shows the record's own metadata — the same line the History row carries — and its progress through the game as *shown ply / recorded plies*. That pair is the accepted separate move-progress and playback state; there is no side-to-move line, because a finished game is not anybody's turn.
- **That pair is the top of the page in both layout shapes.** Beside the board it is the panel's first section, which is already the top. Beneath the board it is **above** the board rather than under it — where the stacked arrangement puts what is true of the game. The screen is therefore one design in both shapes: what the game was, then the board, then the record and the transport.
- **The room that move frees is shared, and the share leans to the board.** Moving the pair out of the panel does not make the page any shorter, so what the move list gains beneath the board the board pays for above it, point for point. Two things keep that price small: beneath the board the panel asks for **less** than it does beside the board, where nothing is competing with it; and the pair claims **no air below itself** there, because the board's own allowance is already that air. The side-by-side shape pays nothing, because there the pair was always the top of the panel. Where the space is shorter still it is the panel that yields, not the board — the grant under [Layout shapes](#layout-shapes) reserves the board's floor first, and replay could stand on that floor if it had to, its board being a read-only document with no touch target on it.
- **Where the destination bar stands across the bottom, this screen hides it**, which is the general rule in [Navigation](#navigation). What the reclaimed height buys is the **board**: replay's stacked board is bounded by its chrome's height rather than by the width — the progress pair, the transport and the resident move list are granted their room before the board is fitted — so the hidden bar's height goes to the board, on a 402-point iPhone lifting Mini Xiangqi to pitch 52, while the move list keeps its usable rows. The full-width pitches belong to the play board, which carries no resident list.
- The accepted history orientation and visible Flip Board control remain available for the games that have an orientation. A placement record has none, per [Board orientation](#board-orientation): its replay opens as the board is drawn, and the cluster is the transport alone.
- Pin, Share, and Delete are managed from the History list rather than from replay.
- **A step travels; a jump cuts.** One ply forward or back is shown with the same distance-scaled travel a played move uses — a step back reverses it exactly as an Undo does, restoring what the move took — and its landing performs the same meaning-chosen sound a played landing performs, under the same single sound setting: a replayed landing is a landing, and gets no rule of its own. A jump of more than one ply, and a step asked for while one is still travelling, are presentational cuts instead — nobody asking for the twentieth position wants to watch nineteen moves go past — and a cut has no landing, so it is silent.
- Autoplay starts only after a user action and waits for each move animation to finish before advancing.
- **Autoplay runs at one speed.** The 0.5×/1×/2× set is deferred rather than withdrawn: a speed control is three more controls on a transport that has five already, plus a preference to carry. Manual move navigation, flipping the board, or the app moving to the background pauses playback. Playback stops at the final position.
- With Reduce Motion, replay uses the accepted crossfade or immediate board update while preserving the same order and playback controls.

**On Windows replay is the step-through half of this section and no more.** The transport carries **four** controls rather than five — jump to beginning, one move back, one move forward, jump to end — and the two clauses above that describe playing a game back on its own are unimplemented rather than reinterpreted: there is no autoplay, therefore no pause, no speed, and nothing for a manual navigation or a background transition to interrupt. **A step cuts there too.** The travel clause is motion language; a Windows step is the presentational cut a jump already is, and it is silent for the same reason a cut is. Everything the section says about what replay *shows* is unchanged and is what the viewer is: it begins at the initial position, the board is read-only, the move list highlights the shown move and jumps to a selected one, the metadata line is the row's own, the progress pair is *shown ply / recorded plies*, the orientation rule and its visible control stand, and Pin, Share and Delete stay on the list.

### History library

- Pinned records appear before unpinned records. Each group orders the most recently recorded or imported first. **That order is the core's**, and the list never re-sorts it: the two groups are the pinned prefix of what the store returned and the rest of it.
- The two groups are **rendered as two sections**, **Pinned** and **Other Games**, rather than as a per-row pin badge. Pinned-ness is ordering rather than decoration, and rendering it as ordering keeps a glyph off a row that is already carrying five facts and gives pin and unpin a visible confirmation for free — the row moves between the sections, and a pin the store refused is a row that did not move. With nothing pinned there is one unheaded section and the list reads as a plain list of games.
- Each entry shows its date, game, mode, result or end reason, and move count. Human-versus-AI entries also show the human side; imported records have a visible imported marker.
- **The row is two lines.** The first is when the game ended. The second is the accepted metadata composition applied to a filed game — `game · mode · [side] · result · [end reason] · move count` — which is the save-and-continue confirmation's own vocabulary rather than a second one invented here. The mixed library remains one pinned-first chronological list: game identity is a metadata fact, not a filter, badge, or third grouping. The second line never truncates, so every required token wraps rather than disappearing.
- Two segments are left out where the line would otherwise say one thing twice. **side** is absent in Free Play, where the turn status omits a controller label for the same reason. **end reason** is absent where the result word already carries it, which is exactly the ended-early record: `outcome = none` holds exactly when `end_reason = ended-early`, so the row shows **Ended Early** in the result slot and says it once. A resignation keeps its reason, because **Red Wins** alone does not say whether the loser was mated or resigned.
- **The date is the game's own end**, in the reader's locale, calendar and time zone: the system's *today* or *yesterday* word plus the time for those two days, and a numeric date with a four-digit year plus the time otherwise. The time is always present, because it is what tells two games played on one day apart, and **no date or time pattern is ever written by the app** — whether the clock reads 14:32 or 2:32 PM belongs to the locale and to the reader's own system setting.
- **The move count is the integer, a space, and the unit** — `42 moves`. It counts plies, which is what the Chinese unit 步 counts; the English *moves* takes the ordinary-English sense and Help owes the definition.
- Selecting an entry opens its read-only replay.
- Record content is read-only. Pinning changes only local library organization.
- A partial right-to-left swipe reveals icon-and-text actions on the trailing side. From left to right, they are blue **Share** and red **Delete**, with Delete nearest the trailing edge.
- A complete right-to-left swipe invokes Delete.
- A left-to-right swipe reveals **Pin** or **Unpin**. A complete swipe invokes that reversible action immediately.
- History has no Move action because there are no folders or tags.
- **Share** exports the selected History record as one game file.
- **Confirm Before Deleting** is a Settings toggle and is enabled by default. When enabled, either the visible Delete action or a complete swipe presents:
  - Title: **Delete this game?**
  - Message: **This game can't be recovered.**
  - Actions: **Cancel** and **Delete**.
- The record remains in the list until the user confirms. When **Confirm Before Deleting** is disabled, either deletion gesture permanently deletes immediately. **Every** Delete entry point is gated identically — the swipe action, the complete swipe, the context menu, and the screen-reader custom action — because they are the same operation and the confirmation is about the operation rather than about the gesture.
- There is no deletion Undo and no Recently Deleted collection. If persistence fails, the record remains and the app presents the same "could not do it, nothing changed, try again" alert the rest of the app uses: title **Couldn't Delete This Game**, message **This game is still in History. Please try again.**, actions **Cancel** and **Try Again**.
- **A pin or unpin the store refuses is not an alert.** It is the accepted non-blocking treatment for a reversible, low-stakes action, and the two sections are what report it: the row does not move.
- **An empty History says so quietly** — **No Games Yet**, with **Games you finish are saved here.** beneath it — and offers nothing else to do. A library that cannot be read says *that* instead, in the failure-screen family, rather than showing an empty list it has no evidence for.
- Import selects one game file at a time. A valid import creates an immutable History record and leaves the active game unchanged.
- An exact duplicate does not create a second record and offers a way to view the existing record. A stable-identity conflict with different game content is rejected with an explanation.
- **Import lives on this destination and nowhere else** — a toolbar item, **Import…** — because this is the screen the result lands on. The picker allows exactly one file and filters to the declared game type, which is where the one-file-at-a-time rule is kept rather than where it is enforced by refusal. The app reads the bytes inside the security scope the picker grants and keeps no bookmark and no reference afterwards, which is the right posture for input the repository calls untrusted. It checks the file's size before reading it and declines a file over the accepted 1 MiB bound without allocating for it; the bound itself is the core's, and the core is still what enforces it. The empty state gains no import button: it says what it says and offers nothing else to do, and the toolbar item is above it either way.
- **A successful import says nothing.** The row appears at the head of the unpinned group — its History-added time is now — the list scrolls it into view, and it carries a brief highlight that decays. The answer is the row, and an alert about something already on screen would be an alert that trains people to dismiss alerts. Under Reduce Motion the scroll arrives immediately; the highlight is colour and is unchanged.
- **Everything else an import can answer is an alert**, because every one of them is a thing that did not happen. The classes are the ones the core's own taxonomy distinguishes, and the file's own diagnostic text is never shown:
  - a **duplicate** — a success that deliberately does not meet the expectation a first import sets — with title **This Game Is Already in History**, message **The game in this file is identical to one already in History, so it wasn't added again.**, and actions **View**, which opens the existing record's replay, and **OK**;
  - an **identity conflict**, with title **This File Conflicts with a Game in History** and message **It is the same game as one in History, but its contents differ. History is unchanged. To use this file, delete that game from History first.**, and **OK**. The message names the only route forward rather than offering it as a button: deleting the existing record is permanent, and it should be reached deliberately;
  - a file **created by a newer version**, with title **This File Was Created by a Newer Version of Star River** and message **This version can't read it. Update Star River and try again. History is unchanged.**, and **OK**. This is the one message the data contract requires to be distinct, and no word in it means corrupt;
  - a file that **cannot be read** — every other refusal the archive domain has, from a wrong file to a damaged one to an oversized one — with title **Can't Read This Game File** and message **The file's contents are invalid or too large to import. History is unchanged. Check that the file is complete, or ask for a new copy.**, and **OK**;
  - a file whose game **starts from a position the rules refuse** — a readable file, but its recorded start is not a position its game may be set up in — with title **This Game Starts from a Position the Rules Don't Allow**, message **The file is readable, but the game in it begins from a position these rules don't allow, so it can't be imported. History is unchanged.**, and **OK**. It offers no retry, because the rules' answer for this file will not change;
  - a **damaged record already under this file's identity**, with title **A Game in History Is Damaged** and message **The record in History matching this file is damaged, so it can't be compared or imported. To use this file, delete that game from History first.**, and **OK**. This is the one import answer that is about the library rather than about the file, and the only one that may say *damaged* — the record's own bytes no longer decode or no longer match the hash recorded for them, so the comparison an import has to make cannot be made. It offers no retry, because a retry would find the same damage; its route out is the conflict's, and it says so for the same reason;
  - a file that was fine but **could not be saved**, with title **Couldn't Save the Imported Game**, message **The game file is fine, but saving it to History failed. History is unchanged. Please try again.**, and **Cancel** and **Try Again** — the same "could not do it, nothing changed, try again" shape the rest of the app uses.
- All but two of those messages say **History is unchanged** explicitly, because no persistent change is the guarantee the core makes and a reader has no other way to know it held. The duplicate is one exception: its own sentence already says what became of the library — the game was not added again — so the guarantee would be that fact a second time. The other is the damaged record, which is the one answer that is not about the file: its message names the damaged record and the deletion that is the way past it, and that guarantee there would be a guarantee about a library the message has just called damaged. The strings themselves are the catalog's, per [Localization](#localization).
- **Share exports through the platform's own sharing**, over a file rather than over data alone: the services an offline team actually moves a game with — AirDrop, Mail, Messages — appear only for a file. The filename is built from the game's own end in a fixed, machine-ordered form (`minixiangqi-2026-07-28-1432.mxq`), which is a deliberate and narrow exception to the localization rule: a displayed date belongs to the reader, and a filename belongs to the file. It is the game's end and not the export event because the export event is regenerated every time, and the same game would otherwise arrive under a different name on every share.
- Bulk deletion, search, filters, tags, and editing a History game are absent.
- Trackpad swipes use the same leading and trailing behavior where supported. Pointer context menus, keyboard commands, and screen-reader custom actions — VoiceOver on Apple platforms, Narrator on Windows — expose equivalent Pin or Unpin, Share, and Delete operations without adding permanent row buttons. On Windows, the context menu is the primary path to these actions.
- Action meaning is carried by icon and text as well as color.

**Two clauses read differently on Windows, and both are the platform answering rather than the design changing.**

- **The date is the reader's locale numeric date and time on every day, including today and yesterday.** Neither formatter a Windows app composes dates with has a relative form: .NET's globalization data has none, and WinRT's `DateTimeFormatter` has short and long dates and nothing between them. The shell has one — `SHFormatDateTime` with `FDTF_RELATIVE` — and it is a documented public entry point an unpackaged app can call, but it is the wrong source twice over: it formats from the **user locale** rather than from the language the app is running in, so a Chinese-language app on an English system would get *Today* in the middle of a Chinese row, and it accepts no format control, so the four-digit year this clause asks for cannot be obtained from it. Writing a *today* of our own into the string table would be the app supplying a word this clause assigns to the system, and inventing the day boundary it depends on; dropping the time would give up the one thing the relative branch exists to keep. So Windows takes the section's other branch on every day, and the time is present throughout. Nothing else changes: no date or time pattern is written by the app there either, both halves being the culture's own short date and short time, with a two-digit year widened to four because this clause asks for four.
- **Share writes the file through the system's Save As rather than through a share sheet.** The clause's own reasoning is that sharing must go over a file rather than over data alone, because the services an offline team moves a game with appear only for a file. On Windows, `DataTransferManager`'s share UI reaches installed share targets and is not where anyone goes to hand somebody a file; the platform's conventional equivalent — and therefore what [Platform adaptation](#platform-adaptation)'s rule requires — is to write the file, from which every one of those services can then take it. The file, its bytes, its accepted name and its extension are identical either way, which is the part the interchange promise is about. A refusal is the save dialog's own to report and the app adds no second alert behind it.

## Help

Help is the education surface and is deliberately small:

- Help is a read-only Mini Xiangqi rules reference covering the board, the pieces and their movement, check and checkmate, stalemate, repetition and the claimable draw, perpetual check, and perpetual chase, plus a short explanation of the app's own controls.
- Help is reachable from Settings and from the game screen without abandoning or pausing state: opening help never modifies the active game, and returning restores the exact prior context.
- Help content is static reference material. It does not analyze the current position, suggest moves, or offer interactive lessons or drills.
- Help text follows the same localization requirements as the rest of the interface.

The exact entry points, content organization, and illustrations are settled with the Help content itself.

## Motion and visual effects

Animation, motion, and visual effects are part of the intended experience. They must communicate state changes and preserve the user's understanding of the position. Reduced-motion preferences and interruption behavior are designed alongside the default experience.

The motion language is restrained and tactile:

- Selecting a piece uses a brief, approximately 120–160 ms lift, scale, and shadow transition without continuous movement.
- Empty legal destinations use a small dot; capturable destinations use a dashed ring around the target piece. The two states differ by shape and do not rely on color alone. Because a piece style may itself ring a disc, the capture ring must stay clearly distinguishable from any ring belonging to the piece style, in every style; the separation rule in [Board metrics](#board-metrics) is what guarantees it.
- During a drag, the piece follows the pointer or touch, its origin retains a subtle marker, and a nearby legal target strengthens its feedback.
- An ordinary move travels smoothly to its destination in approximately 180–240 ms.
- A capture coordinates a brief scale-and-fade removal with the moving piece's arrival and targets an overall duration of approximately 250 ms.
- An invalid drop returns the piece smoothly to its origin and gives the same brief feedback as an illegal tap, without an alert or forceful shake.
- An AI move uses the same move language and leaves persistent origin and destination markers so the player can identify the completed move.
- Check uses a non-color-only treatment on the checked general plus one brief pulse, defined under [Game-state markers](#game-state-markers). It does not flash continuously.
- Undo visually reverses the affected move or decision cycle and restores a captured piece when needed. Board input and another Undo remain unavailable until the transition completes, and a new action does not interrupt a running transition. Because repeated Undo is an accepted capability, an Undo transition must therefore complete within 250 ms for one ply and 600 ms for a decision cycle, so that walking a game back does not accumulate noticeable waiting.
- Board flipping uses an approximately 300–400 ms coordinated re-layout while piece text and symbols remain upright.
- With Reduce Motion, the animation of lifts, springs, pulses, long-distance travel, and the suggested capture's dash drift is removed in favor of a brief crossfade or immediate state update. The states themselves remain: a held piece still reads as raised, it simply arrives at that appearance without an animated transition.

**Move travel scales with distance, inside the accepted band.** A chariot crossing the board and a general stepping one point are not the same event, so travel time rises with distance within the band accepted above. The mapping is a chosen proportion, not a derived quantity, and it must respect the accepted ceiling on an Undo of a decision cycle.

**The AI's move has a floor, not a delay.** Its piece departs at the later of two instants: when the search returns, and a short fixed interval after the player's own move has finished animating — the arrival, not the tap that committed it, since the AI must not leave before the player's move has finished being shown. A search of a second or more is unaffected; only a near-instant reply waits, so the AI never appears to twitch rather than move. AI activity appears in the turn status only once a search has run long enough for an indicator to be worth showing.

**Motion runs at the display's own rate.** The application caps no animation's frame rate: where the hardware offers a faster refresh, travel, the flip, the pulses and the dash drift all take it, and a display that manages its own rate is left to manage it. What a clock-driven schedule bounds is *when* it runs — the drift's timeline is paused whenever no suggested capture stands — never how often it ticks.

**Interruption divides in two.** A *presentational* transition — a lift, a hover, a marker appearing — re-targets freely toward whatever the player just did. A *committing* transition — a move, a capture, an Undo — runs to completion, and input arriving during it is **discarded rather than queued**, so a player never watches a stack of actions replay. Board flipping is the one action deferred rather than discarded: it changes nothing about the game, so it is applied when the running transition ends.

**Reduce Motion is one rule.** Anything that animates position, scale, or rotation becomes a brief crossfade; anything that animates opacity, colour, stroke weight, or shadow is unchanged, because none of those is motion; any spring that survives loses its overshoot rather than its duration; and the order in which things happen is untouched. The check pulse is removed rather than converted, as the accepted rule requires of every pulse: check is a persistent treatment plus a pulse, so the ring and the **Check** token still say everything the pulse said. The suggested capture's dash drift is removed rather than converted for the same reason: the suggestion is its standing marks plus a drift, and the marks say everything the drift says. Every state survives — a held piece still reads as raised, a checked general still carries its rings, the legal destinations still answer an illegal tap — they simply arrive without travel.

**Feedback that reports an event fires when the event completes**: a move sounds when the piece lands, not when it lifts. **Feedback that answers a touch leads its animation** — selection, an illegal tap, refused input, and a failed save all respond at the touch rather than at the end of whatever is drawn in reply, because the touch is what the player is waiting to feel answered.

Exact durations, easing, scale factors, shadow, opacity, and feedback strength are settled on physical iPhone, iPad, and Mac hardware, within the bands accepted above; a change to an accepted duration is a contract change rather than a tuning pass. Board-state markers are drawn directly and never become translucent decoration; where Liquid Glass may appear is fixed under [Platform visual language](#platform-visual-language).

## Sound and haptics

Sound and haptics are part of the intended experience. They must reinforce meaningful actions and game events and must never be the only way information is conveyed.

Both are user-controllable through separate Settings toggles, per the Settings scope in [product.md](product.md). Haptics are available only where the hardware provides them; on a device without them the toggle is unavailable rather than silently ineffective, and no substitute effect is invented.

**The two toggles are presented together, and each is a plain switch.** They are one group of the Settings destination, with no group header — **Sound** above **Haptics** — and neither is nested under or conditioned on the other, because they are independent preferences of equal standing: sound off leaves a landing felt, and haptics off leaves it heard. Each takes effect at the next event rather than at the next launch. **Settings itself is silent**: sound is an event of the board, and a screen that clicked back at every switch would be the app talking about itself.

**On macOS the haptics toggle is offered.** The hardware question there belongs to the trackpad rather than to the machine — a Mac may have a Force Touch trackpad built in, attached, or neither, and that can change while the app is running — and the system haptic performer already honours each machine's own trackpad, doing nothing where there is none. So "unavailable rather than silently ineffective" is satisfied by the platform itself, and a toggle the app greyed out by guessing at hardware would be a worse answer than the system's own.

**On iOS and iPadOS the switch is offered where the engine is there and is absent where it is not.** The question is asked of the device rather than inferred from it: Core Haptics' own `supportsHaptics` capability, which is the answer Apple's guidance says to read before playing haptics and which names iPad among the devices that do not support haptic feedback. A list of model names would be a guess that goes stale on the next device, and a feedback generator on a device with no engine does nothing at all rather than failing, so nothing later in the app can discover the answer — it has to be asked before the switch is drawn. Where the answer is no the row is **removed rather than greyed out**: this is not a state the device can leave, so a permanently disabled switch would be a control that can never be reached, and explaining it would mean a third footer on a screen that deliberately carries two. iOS's own Settings does the same thing, showing no haptic rows at all on a device without the engine. The group survives losing the row: it has no header to strand, and the two switches were never conditioned on each other, so **Sound** standing alone means exactly what it meant standing above **Haptics**.

**On Windows the switch is not offered, and the group is Sound alone.** The row is **removed rather than greyed out**, which is iOS's answer, but the grounds are different and are worth stating exactly, because the obvious reason is false. **Windows does have a touchpad counterpart to the Mac's system haptic performer**: `Windows.Devices.Haptics.InputHapticsManager`, added in UniversalApiContract 19 with Windows 11 24H2, whose `HapticDeviceType` names Touchpad and Mouse and which answers `IsSupported()` and `IsHapticDevicePresent()` before `TrySendHapticWaveform` plays anything. The reason the switch is absent is that **it is not for the MVP**: the API is experimental and gated to 24H2 and later, and the hardware that carries it is rare, so the app would be shipping a switch that does nothing on nearly every machine it runs on — which is what "unavailable rather than silently ineffective" forbids just as surely as no API at all would. The group survives losing the row for the reasons already given. The key is not written either: `haptics.enabled` is left exactly as an Apple frontend stored it. **If the switch is ever offered here, no new rule is needed** — iOS's is already the right one. "The question is asked of the device rather than inferred from it" is exactly what `IsSupported()` plus `IsHapticDevicePresent()` is in Windows' own words, and the same answer follows: where the answer is no the row is removed.

**The board is heard on Windows in the same places and by the same rule**, which is the whole of what the four voices owe this platform: one sound per landing, chosen by what the landing means, in the accepted order of precedence, with the conclusion sounding at the commit for the two results that arrive with nothing moving. The samples are the same four files. There is no silent switch to obey and no audio session to declare — an unpackaged desktop app has no platform suspension, and losing focus is not one, exactly as it is not one on macOS — so a game still being played is still heard, and the system's own volume mixer is where somebody who wants it quieter goes. **Windows replay is silent**, per the Windows clause under [History replay](#history-replay): a step there is the presentational cut a jump already is, and a cut has no landing.

**On iPhone and iPad the ring/silent switch silences the board, and the haptics it does not touch.** The sounds play through the `ambient` audio session category, whose own definition is this section's first sentence written by somebody else — the category for an app in which sound playback is nonprimary, one that also works with the sound turned off — and which both the silent switch and the lock screen silence. Somebody who has silenced their phone has already said what they want from the room they are in, and a board that knocked anyway would be overruling them in order to say something it has also said on the board; obeying costs nothing, because no sound here is the only channel for anything. The category **mixes** as well: a learner's own music, lesson or podcast keeps playing, where the platform's default category would have stopped it the moment the board appeared, and a board that silences a podcast to make room for one tock has its priorities backwards. The felt half does not pass through the audio session at all, so a silenced phone still lands a move in the hand — the two channels staying independent, exactly as their two switches are — unless the system's own Play Haptics in Silent Mode setting says otherwise, which is the platform's answer to give and not ours. macOS has no such switch and its sounds follow the system volume.

Haptic strength follows the meaning of the event, not its frequency. In particular, tapping an illegal square is a normal part of learning how the pieces move rather than a failure, so it uses the platform's lightest selection-weight feedback and never the system warning pattern. The warning pattern is reserved for genuine failures such as an action that could not be saved, and overusing it would both punish the learner and dilute its meaning. The two must remain distinguishable from each other, as their visual responses already are: an illegal tap answers on the board by strengthening the legal destinations, while a failed save appears only at the turn status.

### Board play

**The board plays one sound per landing, and chooses it by what the landing means.** A real set has one dominant sound — a piece meeting the board — so that is the sound the app is built around, and the rest are variations on it rather than additions to it. The five:

- **A plain landing** is a quiet, wooden, percussive knock. Every committing arrival makes it, an Undo's return included: taking a move back is a piece landing on a point, and the board already shows the reversal.
- **A stone landing** is the plain landing of a board that places: the same knock, sharper and smaller. A stone is a harder, lighter thing than a wooden disc, and it arrives without travelling.
- **A capture** is the same knock, lower and slightly weightier. A take is a landing with mass.
- **A check** is the plain knock plus one brighter, quieter accent transient, sounding with the check rings' pulse.
- **A conclusion** is a soft, low, settling gesture. It **replaces** the landing sound rather than joining it, and it is one neutral sound for every result: which result it was is said on screen, in the notice and in the turn status. A draw the player claims ends the game without any piece moving, so its conclusion sounds at the moment the claim is confirmed.

Where a landing answers to more than one of these, the higher meaning is the only one heard: **conclusion, then capture, then check, then the plain landing** — a stone's landing being the plain one of its own board, so a placement game's conclusion replaces it exactly as it replaces a disc's. A capturing check therefore sounds like a capture — the take is the louder fact, and the check already has the rings, the **Check** token, and the status line saying it.

**Sound character is percussive, dark, and short**: a landing's sound is an impact rather than a tone, nothing melodic is used — a sequence of pitches reads as interface decoration rather than as a board — and the overall level stays under the system alert sound at the same volume, quiet enough to sit beneath the landing haptic rather than compete with it. The samples are generated in-repo from a checked-in script rather than sourced, so the sound is tuned by parameter and regenerated, and the app carries no third-party audio. Exact spectra, decay times, and levels are settled by ear against the running app, as motion's exact durations are settled against running hardware.

**Silence is a decision too.** Nothing sounds for an illegal tap, refused input, a selection or a lift, a board flip, or the acknowledgment beat. Those are answered on the board and by the platform's lightest haptic: an exploratory tap is how the moves are learned, and a noise in reply would read as a reprimand.

**The haptic set is separate and is not derived from the sound set.** Every landing takes the alignment pattern, whatever the landing meant; the touch answers take the lightest pattern. A result changes what is heard and not what is felt.

## Accessibility

The interaction design must cover:

- VoiceOver and meaningful labels, values, actions, and reading order.
- Keyboard interaction and focus behavior where supported.
- Sufficient contrast and state cues that do not rely only on color.
- Dynamic Type and text legibility.
- Reduce Motion, Reduce Transparency, and other relevant platform settings.
- Alternatives for information otherwise communicated through sound, haptics, or animation.

**A point's description is composed from the square name and then the board's own vocabulary in that order** — the side, the piece, and the point's state — with the piece named by its character in Chinese and by its English name in English, per [Piece representation](#piece-representation).

## Localization

The supported languages are Simplified Chinese and English. Simplified Chinese is the source language: the accepted Simplified Chinese is normative, and its English counterparts are translations of it. **The project's development region and each catalog's own source language stay `en` all the same**, because that normativity is a property of this contract rather than of an Xcode setting, and nothing about the build turns on it.

Every string the interface shows lives in its own frontend's string table, in both languages: the String Catalogs `apple/MiniXiangqi/Localizable.xcstrings` and `InfoPlist.xcstrings`, and on Windows `windows/MiniXiangqi.Play/Text/Strings.cs`. Each is the string of record for the frontend it belongs to, which is why a string only one of them draws is registered only there: the strings quoted throughout this document describe behavior, and where a quotation and a table entry disagree, the entry is the copy.

**`InfoPlist.xcstrings` carries what the system shows on the application's behalf**, in both languages: the application's own name, both as the name the system displays for it and as the short name macOS titles its app menu with; the kind the system gives the exported game file; and the sentence inside the local-network permission alert. A string is registered under a symbolic key rather than under its own source text, except there: a value the system reads out of the bundle's plist is keyed by that value's own source text, and a plist key the system localizes is keyed by the key.

The app follows the language the operating system selects for it and offers no interface-language control of its own, per the Settings scope in [product.md](product.md). On Apple platforms the system's per-app language setting is the place a user changes it; on Windows the app follows the system's language preference list.

The interface must be designed for localization. User-facing text must not be embedded in visual assets, and layouts must tolerate different text lengths. Terminology for Xiangqi pieces, rules, results, and controls must be consistent within each supported language.

Piece characters are game content and are excluded from localization, as defined under [Piece representation](#piece-representation). Their English names localize wherever they appear as text.

**Two things the interface shows are not copy and are not localized**: the technical description beneath a failure screen's title, which is diagnostic text from the core, and the licence text the About page shows, which is the GNU General Public License's own document.

## Orientation and layout

### Orientation

- **iPhone runs in portrait only.** Apple's guidance permits an experience that runs in a single orientation, and relies on people simply trying both and settling on the one that works; the app therefore never asks the user to rotate the device and shows no message about orientation.
- **iPad supports every orientation**, because iPadOS expects an app to adapt to rotation and to being windowed at arbitrary sizes, and an app that declines rotation opts out of that behaviour.
- macOS has no orientation; the window's proportions select the layout in the same way a device orientation does.
- Locking iPhone to portrait has a cost worth naming: a user whose device is fixed in a mount, or whose grip or dexterity favours landscape, cannot compensate by rotating. Portrait gives the board the full screen width and gives Xiangqi's taller grid the long axis; the accessibility obligation is met through the input alternatives already accepted rather than through rotation.
- Windows devices that rotate follow the same space-driven layout rules.

### Layout shapes

Two arrangements cover every device and window size, chosen by the available **space** rather than by device identity, so a resized Mac window and a multitasking iPad behave the same way as each other.

- **Stacked**, used by iPhone portrait, by iPad portrait, and by any window that is narrow for its height — a half-screen Mac window included: the turn status in the bar's centre per [Turn status](#turn-status), the play controls below the board in a slot reserved at their tallest arrangement, and the board centred in the height that slot leaves.
- **Side by side**, used by iPad landscape, wide iPad window sizes, and Mac windows with width to spare, which is most of them: the board on one side with a panel beside it carrying the turn status, the move list, game metadata, and controls that do not need to sit under the thumb.

**The rule is which arrangement gives the selected game's board more.** Both shapes fit the same board profile into the same rectangle and differ only in what they take out first: side by side takes the panel's width, stacked takes the chrome's height. Whichever leaves the larger pitch is used, and a tie goes to side by side, which shows the move list for free. A shape that cannot draw the selected game at its floor loses to one that can.

**It is the available space and never a width threshold.** The two shapes' costs are on different axes, so no single width can express the rule: the accepted macOS minimum window is bound by its **height** and takes side by side at 626 points of content, while an iPad in portrait is bound by nothing and takes stacked at 834, and one threshold would have to be both below 626 and above 834.

The game profile is an input to that one rule, not a second layout. At the same width, Xiangqi uses nine pitches where Mini Xiangqi uses seven and its ten-rank block is taller. A Mac window that is tall and narrow still stacks, and a wide window still goes side by side when the panel costs the board no pitch. Near a boundary the win may be one point of pitch; tests pin the exact sizes where each profile changes answer rather than adding a device or game threshold.

The board is sized to the largest instance of its own aspect ratio fitting **both** the available width and the height left after the surrounding chrome, so neither game overflows a short window. Mini Xiangqi never falls below a 44-point pitch. Xiangqi keeps the same width footprint and never falls below a 34-point pitch: `7 × 44 = 308`, while the largest whole-point nine-file pitch inside that footprint is `34`, for a 306-point core. The placement boards take the same footprint divided by fifteen files and never fall below a 20-point pitch, for a 300-point core. Those are real cell sizes. Each hit element occupies its own cell and no invisible 44-point frame overlaps its neighbours.

**A stacked board screen is fitted to the full width.** The shape spends no horizontal allowance on a board screen: the block takes the space's full width — the screen's on a phone, the window's in a narrow Mac window — the whole-point pitch leaves a few points of remainder at most, and the style's board surface runs to the space's edges beneath the numeral strips, so the board meets the edge without a sliver of page beside it. On an iPhone that is what the shape exists to buy — a 402-point screen carries Xiangqi at pitch 44 — while an iPad in portrait is stopped by the maximum footprint below. The board's air in this shape is above and below it, where the height is. A pre-start preview keeps its air: it sits among setup controls on a page rather than alone on a screen, and the full-width rule is the board screens'. The fitting is the Apple frontend's; Windows, whose coordinate strips stand on all four edges, keeps its own derived fitting until the board-page catch-up the register records.

At their floors, Mini Xiangqi's core is 308 points square, Xiangqi's is 306 by 340, and a placement core is 300 square with its rank-number strip beside it. Width fitting is what keeps Xiangqi's taller block on a phone screen rather than off it. The smallest supported Mac display and largest-text setting remain running-frontend gates for the board, chrome and longer metadata together.

**The board has the same maximum width footprint in every game.** Mini Xiangqi stops at pitch 102 and a 714-point core; Xiangqi stops at pitch 79 and a 711-point-wide core; the placement boards stop at pitch 47 and a 705-point core. Whole-point pitches keep every derived dimension stable. Surplus space goes to the surrounding layout, and the half-cell margin stays functional space rather than a spacer to inflate.

**In the stacked shape the board's frame does not follow the controls.** The cluster's slot is reserved at its tallest arrangement, so which arrangement the cluster draws and which scene it is in — one row or two, a running game's controls or a finished one's, this side of a nearby turn or the other — changes neither where the board sits nor how large it is. The controls are anchored at the bottom of the slot and the spare height is air between them and the board.

When space is short the surrounding chrome tightens before the board does. That preference has a floor: the board may not be driven below the sizes above, and neither may the chrome be driven below what its own controls require. Each platform therefore defines a minimum window size that keeps both above their floors, and the window stops resizing there rather than either becoming unusable.

**The Apple play content's shared floor is 626 by 416 points.** Width is governed by the placement board, whose floor block is the widest: a 300-point core and the rank-number strip beside it, plus 260 points of panel and 24 points of air on each side. Height is governed by Xiangqi: its 34-point pitch produces a 306-by-340 core and a 368-point block with the two numeral strips; the same air makes 416. Taking the maximum on each axis lets one window switch games without resizing or shrinking any of them below its floor — and the floor is a bound rather than a target: a game that governs neither axis can sit above its own floor at the minimum window rather than on it, which at this floor Mini Xiangqi does, one point above 44.

**macOS stops at 770 by 520 points**, which gives the 626-by-416 play-content floor inside the measured 144-point sidebar, 52-point toolbar and title-bar chrome. The number moves if the navigation container's measured presentation changes.

**That floor belongs to the platforms with a resizable window**, which means macOS and Windows. A minimum size is a thing that stops a resize, and iOS and iPadOS have no resize to stop: the screen is the size it is, and a multitasking iPad is sized by the system rather than by the app. A 626-point minimum on a 440-point phone would not widen anything — it would only declare content wider than the screen, which is what the stacked shape exists to avoid.

**Windows derives its resizable-window floor from the same two game profiles and their 44/34-point pitch floors.** Its coordinate strips occupy all four edges while that frontend shows canonical moves, so `windows/MiniXiangqi.Play/Board/BoardSpace.cs` derives the block and shell dimensions rather than copying Apple's two-strip numbers. The minimum is the maximum required by either game, and the navigation pane may overlay but may never squeeze a board below its profile's floor.

**And under every floor, a board that cannot be drawn says so rather than showing nothing.** A minimum window is arithmetic, and arithmetic has a residue: a display scale rounded differently, a window frame the minimum is measured across, a container default that moves under a platform update. Where the space genuinely will not hold a board at the accepted pitch floor, no board is drawn — a board below the floor is a decision this design declines to make quietly — and the space it would have taken carries one line asking for a larger window: **The window is too small to fit the board. Please make it larger.** It is not a failure screen and takes none of that family's treatment: nothing went wrong, nothing was lost, and one drag of a window edge puts it right.

**The edge labels are present at every macOS window size**, including the smallest: seven file numerals per edge for Mini Xiangqi, nine for Xiangqi, and on a placement board the fifteen letters along the bottom with the fifteen numbers up the side. The move list's file number is grounded on the board, both strips are shown together, and the selected game's fitting includes their height.

**The panel's sections begin on one edge**, 16 points in from the panel's own, and its material runs to the top of the window rather than stopping below the title bar: the title bar draws its own treatment over whatever lies beneath it, and beneath it lies the panel rather than bare window.

One exception: a pre-start preview is noninteractive and has no touch targets, so it carries no size floor and yields space to the setup controls whenever they need it. The floor exists to protect interaction, and a preview has none to protect.

**On Windows the preview takes the floor anyway**, and the divergence is recorded rather than silent: the exemption exists so a preview can yield to the setup controls, and that frontend's setup controls are a fixed-width panel that never asks for more, so there is nothing to yield to. Sharing the floor there costs the preview nothing and buys one behaviour instead of two, including the says-so state below.

Navigation uses one adaptive container, and the container is the platform's own rather than a presentation the app selects: a tab bar on iPhone, a tab bar on iPad carrying the platform's own control for expanding it into a sidebar, and a sidebar on a Mac at every window size. An iPad in a narrow window therefore gets the same navigation as an iPhone, and the app decides none of it by device identity because it decides none of it at all.

**That also settles whether the user may switch between the two presentations**: the switch is offered where the platform offers it — on iPad — and nowhere else, and the app adds no control of its own. It is the layout shapes that follow the space; the navigation follows the platform, and the two are not the same rule.

### The move list during play

- **The list pairs and numbers plies from the game's own first mover.** A row is Red's cell then Black's, in the order the two were played, the first row is numbered 1, and which side opens is the game's start position's to say: a game whose start has Black to move numbers Black's first ply 1 with that row's Red cell empty, and Red's answer opens the next pair. No mover is read off a ply's parity.
- Where the side-by-side layout applies, the move list is permanently visible in the panel. Its permanence is a property of that shape and not of a platform: a Mac window narrow enough to stack reaches the list the way every stacked screen does, on demand.
- In the stacked layout during ordinary play it is not shown by default and is reached on demand, so neither the board nor the controls give up space to something consulted occasionally.
- **What reaches it is a toolbar item, Moves, over a sheet.** The item sits in the same toolbar the page's own back control is in — the platform's place for something about this page that is not on it — and the sheet opens at half height, because the other half is the board the list is being consulted about. It is dismissible by the platform's own means and by a **Done**; it is a transient the player asked for, which is exactly the surface [Platform visual language](#platform-visual-language) already allows to cover the board. The list inside it is the same list the panel carries, and it stays a record rather than becoming a control: during play no move is selectable, as in the panel.
- Replay is the exception: its accepted behaviour requires the move list to highlight the current move and to allow jumping to a selected one, so replay in the stacked layout shows the move list rather than hiding it, and the surrounding chrome is what tightens to make room.

### Captured pieces

**In every game whose position is wholly public, captured pieces are not displayed.** What remains is visible on the board, and a second inventory would compete with the primary position without changing any action. That is Mini Xiangqi, Xiangqi, Gomoku and Renju, and the reasoning is the whole of why.

**Jieqi displays them, because there the reasoning does not hold: what a capture takes off the board is knowledge, and knowledge is that game's material.** A player learns an identity by taking it, and a surface that showed nothing would be asking them to hold in their head the one thing the game gave them. The surface is two rows of discs, and each part of it says exactly what [jieqi-rules.md](jieqi-rules.md) entitles the player looking at it to know:

- **The two disc rows are the revealed losses** — the player's own on one row, the opponent's on the other — drawn as the board draws those pieces. A piece revealed on the board was public to both players, and taking it changes nothing about that.
- **A hidden loss is a count to its owner and nothing more.** Losing a face-down piece tells its owner that a piece is gone and never which, so on their own side of the surface the count is the whole of what the loss adds — drawn as that many face-down discs standing at the row's end, after the revealed pieces: the face-down disc is already the surface's word for a piece its reader cannot name, and an anonymous tail carries no order worth keeping.
- **A hidden capture is shown whole to its capturer**, standing among that player's discs, because the capture disclosed the identity to them alone. The two players' surfaces are therefore not the same surface, and both are right; Free Play is the one place one person sees both, per [The Jieqi board](#the-jieqi-board).
- **A loss that left the board face down says so wherever it is shown whole** — drawn with its symbol partway arrived, the reveal's own axis held, so an open capture and a disclosed one never read as the same disc. The telling marks the capture rather than the reader: the capturer's row carries it live, Free Play's panels carry it throughout, and every count an ending resolves keeps it, so which losses were hidden captures stays readable in a finished game and its replay. How far the symbol stands is settled against the rendered board, as every visual specific here is; a reader who hears the row is told the same fact, the hidden word joined to the piece's name.
- **The end of the game discloses everything to both players**, so every count resolves into the pieces it was counting and the finished surface is one both players read the same. Replay of a finished game shows that disclosed surface throughout, a record being a game already over.

On one player's surface only one of the two hidden classes is ever a count — their own hidden losses, which they may not know. The opponent's hidden losses are the pieces this player took, and those they have already seen.

It is a resident surface of the board page and takes the resident rule with it: it never intersects the board block, and the board keeps its pitch floor before this surface is given room. Its discs have two sizes, each the board's own. Resident beside the board they stand at the concealing board's floor pitch — never smaller than the smallest board their pieces could stand on — and the section extends with its rows, no scroll of its own, the panel's list keeping the remainder on its own scroll. In ordinary play the section is small; a deep endgame on the smallest side-by-side windows can crowd the panel, and that is the accepted trade — the list gives way into its own scroll rather than a grant defending it, a defense worth its mechanism only if rendered screens prove it cramped in use. Reached as a sheet they stand at the pitch a board of the sheet's own width would carry — the same fit arithmetic, bounded by the same floor and ceiling — so the disc and the disc it was on the board are one size. Where the rows stand and how a disclosure arrives are settled against the rendered board, as every other visual specific here is.

**Where the board keeps its floor there is no room to be resident in, and there it is reached instead** — the same answer [the move list](#the-move-list-during-play) gives to the same question, on the same surface. Beside the board it is a section of the panel, resident and always readable. In the stacked shape it is a toolbar item over a sheet, because a surface granted whatever height is left over after the board's floor would defeat the reason it exists: a row of discs nobody can read says less than a row a reader asks for and gets whole.

## Platform adaptation

- iOS should support touch-first play, in the layout shapes accepted above.
- iPadOS should use the available space without requiring a separate product model, through those same shapes.
- macOS should support pointer and keyboard conventions while retaining the same game behavior.
- Windows should follow WinUI 3 and Fluent conventions: pointer and keyboard first, native navigation patterns, context menus, and standard window behavior, with touch equivalents on touch-capable devices.
- Where a platform lacks an interaction idiom used elsewhere — for example, list swipe actions on Windows — the same operations must be exposed through that platform's conventional equivalents, such as context menus, hover controls, and keyboard commands, without changing product capabilities.
- The application does not support multiple main windows.
